[1] Title: Quay Redevelopment Plan Unveiled, Day: March 8, 1994 Content: Harborfield council has unveiled a seventy-page framework for the redundant eastern quays, ending two years of speculation about the site's future. The plan, published on 8 March 1994, proposes a mix of housing, workshops and a tidal park across eleven hectares of former coal wharves. Planning committee chair Councillor Beatrice Wynn described the scheme as the largest single regeneration commitment in the borough's history. The document confirms that the council will retain ownership of the foreshortened grain silo, which is listed, while the warehouses to the north are slated for demolition subject to consent. Officers estimate the build-out at nine years across three phases. A formal consultation runs for ninety days from publication, with objections to be heard in the autumn cycle. The chamber of commerce gave the framework a cautious welcome, while the harbour users' association, whose members still moor two lighters at the coal staithes, said it had not been consulted on the moorings' fate. The quays themselves have stood largely unused since the coal trade moved to the deep-water terminal downriver in the late 1970s.
[2] Title: Council Votes Through Quay Scheme After Marathon Debate, Day: October 17, 1995 Content: Harborfield council last night voted 31 to 12, with four abstentions, to adopt the eastern quays development brief, twenty months after its publication in March 1994. The vote, taken at 11:40 pm on 17 October 1995 after a five-hour debate, follows the public inquiry into objections, which sat for eleven days in the spring. The adopted brief differs from the draft in two respects: the housing quota is raised from six hundred to seven hundred and fifty dwellings, and the tidal park is enlarged by a hectare at the expense of the workshop zone. Councillor Wynn said the changes answered the strongest objections heard at the inquiry. Three amendments seeking to retain the northern warehouses were withdrawn after officers advised that their fabric was beyond economic repair. The harbour users' association failed in its bid to have the lighters' moorings protected by condition, and its secretary said an appeal to the ombudsman over the consultation process was being considered. Developers are to be selected by competitive tender in the new year, with a start on site projected for late 1996.
[3] Title: First Phase of Quays Opens to Public, Day: May 22, 1997 Content: The tidal park, first completed element of the Harborfield eastern quays regeneration, was opened to the public on 22 May 1997 by the deputy mayor in a short ceremony watched by some four hundred residents. The opening comes fourteen months after work began on site in March 1996, a start delayed six weeks by the relocation of a colony of sterns nested in the silo's loading gantry. The first housing blocks, of which two hundred and forty dwellings are now under contract, are expected to be ready in the spring of 1998. Council officers confirmed that the development brief adopted in October 1995 remains the governing document, and that the enlarged park delivered exactly the extra hectare won at the inquiry. A permanent home for the harbour users' association's two lighters has yet to be settled; the vessels have been moved to a temporary berth at the marine yard, and the association says it will not quit that berth until a written undertaking is given. The second phase, including the workshops, goes to tender in the autumn.

[1] Title: Northgate Pedestrian Zone Consultation Launches, Day: January 12, 1999 Content: A three-month consultation on pedestrianising Northgate, the borough's congested market street, begins on 12 January 1999, the council has announced. The proposals would remove through traffic from the street between the abbey gatehouse and the corn exchange, resurface the carriageway in york stone, and relocate the twice-weekly market stalls to a widened footway on the eastern side. Officers project a twenty-eight month programme from a decision to completion. Traders have reacted warily; the market federation's chair, Ruby Osei, said her members needed guarantees about compensatory loading arrangements before the closure of the street to vans before eight in the morning. The consultation documents include traffic counts taken over four Wednesdays in November 1998, showing peak-hour flows of nine hundred vehicles. A series of public exhibitions is scheduled for February in the library annexe. The scheme, costed at six and a half million pounds, is contingent on a transport grant decision expected in the summer. No start date has been fixed, and the council stresses that nothing will proceed without a second round of statutory consultation later in the year.
[2] Title: Northgate Works Begin After Grant Confirmation, Day: April 3, 2001 Content: Contractors moved onto Northgate on 3 April 2001, twenty months after the transport grant that made the scheme possible was confirmed in August 1999. The delay was caused by the need to re-route the culverted stream beneath the carriageway, a complication uncovered during trial holes dug in the spring of 2000. The works order, sealed in January 2001, closes the street to all traffic except emergency vehicles for the duration. Market traders have been offered pitch reallocations on the widened eastern footway from the first market day after completion, and a compensation scheme for traders who can show loss during the works closed its applications at the end of March 2001. The council's project board now expects the york stone surfacing to be laid by the winter, with the full scheme, including new lighting and the gatehouse forecourt, complete in the autumn of 2002. Councillors were told last week that the stream diversion has added eight hundred thousand pounds to the budget, absorbed by contingency. The market federation has accepted the loading arrangements negotiated with officers.
[3] Title: Northgate Scheme Opens Amid Trading Row, Day: September 14, 2002 Content: The pedestrianised Northgate was formally opened on 14 September 2002, three years and eight months after the consultation that began the process was launched in January 1999. The opening market, held on the widened eastern footway, drew large crowds, but the event was shadowed by a dispute over compensation: of ninety-three claims submitted under the works scheme, fifty-one have been paid, and the market federation says eleven traders have closed their businesses since the works began in April 2001. The council insists the closures reflect wider retail trends and that every eligible claim assessed has been met. The completed scheme comprises the york stone carriageway, the gatehouse forecourt, and the new lighting columns, delivered one month later than the project board's autumn 2002 projection. Traffic counts taken in the first week show through journeys in the surrounding streets down by a fifth. The transport grant's final instalment is now due, and auditors are to examine the stream diversion costs that stretched the contingency in 2001.

[1] Title: Draft Plan Would Release Green Belt Land, Day: June 9, 2003 Content: The draft unitary development plan published on 9 June 2003 proposes releasing two green belt fields at Weavers' Hill for housing, a departure from the adopted plan of 1996 that is certain to be contested. Together the fields could take one hundred and eighty dwellings. The release is justified by a revised household projection showing the borough needs eleven hundred homes by 2011, a figure the draft acknowledges cannot be met on brownfield land alone. Representations for and against the release must be lodged within six months. The Weavers' Hill protection society, formed within a week of publication, has already engaged counsel and commissioned an ecology survey of the hedgerows. Officers point out that the fields were farmed commercially until 2001 and that their green belt designation, dating from 1975, predates the housing need evidence. The draft also proposes designating the disused canal arm as a wildlife corridor, a measure that has attracted little comment compared with the green belt release. The plan inquiry is expected in late 2004 once representations have been processed.
[2] Title: Weavers' Hill Inquiry Opens, Day: January 20, 2004 Content: The public inquiry into the draft unitary development plan opened on 20 January 2004 at the borough hall, with the green belt release at Weavers' Hill listed as the first and likely longest item. The inspector, appointed in November 2003, sat for the preliminary session with counsel for the council, for the protection society, and for a housebuilder that has promoted an alternative site at Mill Dike. The inquiry heard that representations against the release number one thousand two hundred and forty, the largest single response to any proposal in the plan's history. The council's witness maintained that the household projections left no alternative; the protection society's counsel attacked the projections as dated, pointing to migration figures for 2002 that post-date the draft published in June 2003. Proofs of evidence run to several volumes, and the inquiry is scheduled to sit for four weeks before moving to the plan's other chapters. A decision letter is not expected before the autumn. Meanwhile the canal arm wildlife corridor designation passed through the session unchallenged.
[3] Title: Inspector Backs Smaller Weavers' Hill Release, Day: September 30, 2004 Content: The inspector's report on the unitary development plan, published on 30 September 2004, backs the release of only the western of the two Weavers' Hill fields, finding the eastern field's contribution to green belt openness substantial and the housing case for it not proven. The modified release would allow some seventy dwellings. The inspector also directed that the wildlife corridor along the canal arm be extended to include the eastern field's hedgerow margin. The council accepted the modification without dissent at its meeting of 14 December 2004, adopting the plan and ending eighteen months of contest that began with the inquiry's opening in January 2004. The protection society welcomed the eastern field's reprieve but noted that the western field, farmed until 2001, will now be built on. The housebuilder's Mill Dike alternative was rejected as premature, the inspector holding that its promotion belonged to a future plan review rather than this one. Formal adoption notices were posted on 17 December 2004.
