[1] Title: Smallpox Scare Hastens Hospital Site Purchase, Day: November 14, 1893 Content: The smallpox scare that began with a sailor's landing on 2 November 1893 has hurried the guardians' hand: the board voted on 13 November 1893 to purchase the sanatorium field, eleven acres on the ridge beyond the waterworks, for an isolation hospital, the price four hundred pounds an acre. The town's existing accommodation, four beds in the workhouse's fever shed, was exposed by the scare's nineteen cases in a fortnight, eleven of them sailors' contacts and eight the laundress's household. The field's sellers, the waterworks company, made the purchase conditional on the hospital's drains not entering the waterworks' catchment, a condition the board's surveyor calls impossible and the board accepted anyway. The local government board's sanction for the hospital itself is expected by the spring, the plans to be drawn through the winter. The medical officer's report of the fortnight, published 15 November 1893, credits the laundress's isolation with the cases' containment. The town's papers, unanimous for once, demand the hospital's speed. The scare's last case sickened on 12 November; the sailor who began it has recovered and sailed.
[2] Title: Isolation Hospital Opens With Twelve Beds, Day: September 20, 1898 Content: The isolation hospital, sanctioned in 1894 and built through the spring and summer, opened on 19 September 1898 with twelve beds in two pavilions, the ridge's field now the town's defence. The drains' condition — the impossible one of 1893 — was solved by the surveyor's separate catchpit, its outfall the ridge's own ditch, and the waterworks company's secretary inspected and approved on 12 September 1898. The building's cost, the board's statement says, is six thousand two hundred pounds, the sanction of 1894's estimate having been five thousand eight hundred. The first patient, admitted on the opening day, is a scarlet fever case from the laundress's old street, the hospital's geography already a memory of the scare. The matron and the two nurses appointed in August are lodged in the administration block, its wing the only brick among the pavilions' wood. The medical officer, the report of 1893's author, told the board's visit of 16 September 1898 that the town had waited five years for its twelve beds and must now keep them empty.
[3] Title: Typhoid Tests the Hospital and Finds It Equal, Day: October 11, 1902 Content: The typhoid epidemic that began with the wells of the fair on 14 August 1902 has tested the isolation hospital and, the medical officer's report of 10 October 1902 finds, found it equal: sixty-one cases admitted through the six weeks, the twelve beds turned three times, and a death rate of one in twelve against the region's one in eight. The hospital's pavilions, wooden in 1898, were extended with a third in the spring of 1901, its beds taking the epidemic's overflow, and the fair's wells, the epidemic's source, were closed by the magistrate's order of 22 August 1902. The laundress's street, the scarlet fever's first admission of 1898, sent no typhoid case at all, the street's water having been mains' since the scare year. The matron of 1898's appointing, her four years' service now five and epidemic-hardened, was presented with the board's thanks and a purse on 6 October 1902. The medical officer's report, noting the death rate's mercy, adds the epidemic's arithmetic: sixty-one admitted, five dead, the town's gratitude, and the wells' magistrate's order framed in the board room.

[1] Title: Insurance Act Brings the Dispensary, Day: January 28, 1913 Content: The tuberculosis dispensary, the insurance act's local provision, opened on 27 January 1913 in the converted house on the Chester road, its consulting room, its waiting hall, and its records the town's first concerted weapon against the white plague. The act's machinery — the insurance committee, the panel doctors, the sanatorium benefit — had been running since July 1912, and the dispensary's first week's figures, the medical officer's of 1 February 1913, show forty-one notifications examined, twelve of them referred to the sanatorium benefit's waiting list. The dispensary's tuberculosis officer, a young physician from the Brompton, took up his post on 20 January 1913, his salary half the county's and the town's, the other halves' committees meeting separately to agree it. The waiting list's length, the committee's minutes record, is the sanatorium beds' scarcity, the region's two institutions having four hundred beds among a waiting thousand. The converted house's rent is sixty pounds a year. The town's death rate from the disease, the census of 1911's reckoning, is the region's worst.
[2] Title: War Takes the Physicians, the Dispensary Holds, Day: August 21, 1916 Content: The dispensary's third year finds it the town's steadiest institution: the war has taken its tuberculosis officer and both the panel's junior physicians to the colours, the officer's place held since May 1915 by a woman physician, the first the committee had appointed and the second the town had employed. The notifications of 1916 run at the 1913 rate, the examining room's figures of the August quarter showing thirty-nine, but the waiting list has fallen from a thousand of the region's to the local two hundred, the county's sanatorium of 1914 — forty beds built on the dispensary's evidence — absorbing the town's urgent cases. The insurance committee, meeting 18 August 1916, voted the dispensary's extension into the house's upper floor, the records needing the room, the waiting hall needing the records' emptiness. The woman physician's report, read at the meeting, notes the munitions workers' examinations, the factories' medical officers sending their coughs to the Chester road. The house's rent, sixty pounds of 1913, is held by the landlord's patriotism at the same figure.
[3] Title: Death Rate Falls, the Dispensary's Verdict, Day: March 16, 1920 Content: The tuberculosis death rate's fall, the medical officer's decennial report of 15 March 1920 records, is the town's best statistic: the 1911 census's regional worst has fallen by a third, the dispensary's seven years and the sanatorium's beds and the mains' water all credited. The woman physician of 1915, her war's service now the town's habit, was confirmed in the tuberculosis officer's post on 1 October 1919, the returned physician taking the county's sanatorium instead. The dispensary's upper floor of 1916 is full again, the records' room become the consulting second, and the committee's minutes of 12 February 1920 record the landlord's death and the house's purchase, the sixty pounds' rent become the mortgage's forty. The notifications of 1919 ran at fifty-two, the examining figures' August quarter's munitions coughs now the peace's lingering ones. The death rate's arithmetic, the report's own: 1911's worst, 1914's sanatorium, 1920's fall of a third, and the waiting list's local two hundred of 1916 now the town's chronic sixty. The white plaque's rout, the report concludes, is not won, but the town's weapon is sharpened.

[1] Title: Maternity Home Opens With Six Beds, Day: May 11, 1926 Content: the maternity home, the county's first in the district, opened on 10 May 1926 in the gifted house on the Hospital road, its six beds, its two midwives, and its honorary physician the town's answer to the maternal mortality figures the ministry's circular of March 1926 had tabled. The house, the gift of the mill family whose fortune built the cottage hospital, was accepted by the new trustees on 23 February 1926, its conversion costing eleven hundred pounds. The home's rule — the mothers of the district admitted on the doctor's or the midwife's booking, the fee a shilling a day or the insurance committee's — was posted in the porch. The first booking, the midwife's own niece, was confined on 18 May 1926, the home's first birth its safest advertisement. The honorary physician, the cottage hospital's own, attends the home's Tuesdays and Fridays. The ministry's circular's figures, the maternal death rate's national rise, are the trustees' charter: the district's rate, the physician's estimate, is the nation's equal or worse. The six beds, the trustees' first report of June 1926 says, will be the district's measure.

The maternal mortality figures, the ministry's circular of 1926's charter, remain the nation's anxiety, but the district's confinements in the home rose from the 1926's forty-one to the last year's ninety-eight, the act's intention and the home's habit agreeing. The honorary physician's Tuesdays and Fridays have kept their thirty years' shape — four, now — and his estimate of 1926, the district's rate the nation's equal or worse, is this year's enquiry's question.
