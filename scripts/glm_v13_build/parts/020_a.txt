[1] Title: By-Election Upset as Independent Takes the Seat, Day: February 26, 1974 Content: The by-election of 21 February 1974, caused by the sitting member's elevation to the bench, was won by an independent candidate on a single issue: the town's hospital, slated for closure in the regional board's plan of January 1974. The independent, a surgeon at the very hospital, took the seat with a majority of two thousand four hundred on a swing that humiliated both major parties, the government's candidate falling to third. The count ran to seven hours, the boxes from the mining wards arriving late through the snow. The surgeon's campaign, six weeks from nomination to poll, was run from the hospital's league of friends' tea room, its funds four thousand pounds raised at a hundred-shilling-a-plate dinner. The regional board's chairman said the plan would proceed regardless of one by-election, a sentence the campaign printed on its last leaflet. The government's majority in the house is now three. The surgeon takes the seat as the town's first independent member since 1923, and the hospital's ward windows, the campaign's headquarters' neighbours, kept their lights on him all night.
[2] Title: Independent Member Resigns Over Health Bill, Day: March 14, 1977 Content: The independent member elected in 1974 on the hospital's cause announced his resignation from the house on 11 March 1977, citing the government's health bill's reorganisation clauses, which he says deliver by statute the closure his by-election deferred. The hospital itself stands open still, its surgical wards among the region's busiest, but the bill's regional structure, he told a crowded meeting at the town hall on 12 March 1977, makes the town's defence unwinnable in the new committees. His resignation triggers the third by-election of the parliament. The surgeon's record: three years of the house's oddest alliances, his vote courted by both sides in forty-one divisions, his hospital saved in practice but, he says, lost on paper. The league of friends' tea room, hearing the news, sent him a cake. The government's chief whip, the town's papers report, did not send anything. The writ for the by-election is moved for 18 March 1977, the poll set for 28 April 1977.
[3] Title: Rematch Goes to the Government as Surgeon Stands Again, Day: May 3, 1979 Content: The surgeon, standing again as the independent at the general election of 3 May 1979, was defeated by the government party's new candidate by one thousand one hundred votes, the national tide and the boundary's mining wards' abstentions combining against him. His campaign of 1979 had less to say: the hospital, the bill's reorganisation having failed in the lords' committee in 1978, stands secure, its future written into the region's plan of March 1979 as a district general. The by-election of April 1977, fought on his resignation, had returned a government member with a majority of six hundred, the surgeon not then standing. The health bill of 1977, the resignation's cause, was withdrawn in the autumn of that session. The surgeon's account of his parliamentary years, published this month, is titled "One Issue, Forty-One Divisions". The tea room's cake, he writes, was the house's best hospitality. The new member takes the seat with the hospital's name unmentionable, the town's politics restored to its ordinary colours.

[1] Title: Boundary Commission Proposes to Halve the County, Day: December 12, 1982 Content: The boundary commission's periodic review, published 10 December 1982, proposes to divide the county into two constituencies from the north-west to the south-east, the town and the port sharing one seat and the vale and the moors the other. The county's present three seats, unchanged since 1955's review, would become two, each within the new electorate rules' arithmetic. The town's clerk, reading the maps at the county hall on the 13th, noted that the town's eastern suburbs would vote with the moors, a marriage of mills and heather. The parties' local associations met in emergency through the week, the port's association threatening to campaign against a seat it would share with the town, its rival of a century. The commission's inquiry is set for the town hall on 15 February 1983, objections to be lodged by 31 January. The county's members, two of the three sympathetic to the status quo, have promised the associations their voices. The commission's report is advisory only: the order implementing it must pass the house, and the house, the clerk observes, has a majority of four.
[2] Title: Boundary Order Lives by Two Votes in the Lords, Day: November 6, 1984 Content: The boundary order implementing the county's division passed its lords' reading on 5 November 1984 by two votes, the government's whips having spent a week on the crossbenches, and becomes law by the year's end. The order's parliamentary passage, begun in the spring, was the fight the 1982 review promised: the port's association's campaign, the county's members' voices, and the inquiry of February 1983 all failing to undo the arithmetic. The town's eastern suburbs are indeed married to the moors, the inquiry's one concession a ward exchanged to keep the town's hospital's catchment whole. The new seats, to be fought at the next general election, are named for the town-and-port and the vale-and-moors. The clerk's forecast of the mills-and-heather marriage is now the returning officer's problem. The government's majority, four at the review's publication, was none at the order's reading, the two-vote margin bought with the hospital ward. The associations, beaten, turn to selecting candidates for seats they did not want.
[3] Title: New Seats Fought, Old Habits Survive, Day: June 14, 1987 Content: The general election of 11 June 1987 fought the county's two new seats for the first time, and the results confirmed the review's critics and its authors in equal measure: the town-and-port seat went to the government by three thousand, the vale-and-moors to the opposition by five, the county's aggregate vote almost unchanged from 1983's three-seat totals. The mills-and-heather marriage of the eastern suburbs, the returning officer's problem of 1984, voted as the clerk forecast in 1982 — with the moors, against the town. The hospital ward, the inquiry's concession, split exactly, its ballot boxes recounted once. The port's association's campaign of 1982-83 is remembered as the fight that united the port with the town against both parties, the seat's first member having made the union his theme. The county's politics, the town's editor writes this week, has been halved and survives entire. The boundary commission's next review is not due until the century's turn. The two members, sharing the county hall's former three-room suite, have agreed a rota for its use.

[1] Title: Royal Commission Reports on Local Government, Day: July 22, 1969 Content: The royal commission on local government, reporting on 21 July 1969 after three years of hearings, proposes the county's absorption into a unitary authority spanning town, vale and port, its present councils to be abolished and one council of a hundred members to govern the whole. The commission's chairman, delivering the report at the county hall, called the county's present mosaic — the county council, four boroughs, and twenty-one rural districts — a survival of the 1888 settlement unfit for the century's last third. The town's borough council, at its meeting of 24 July 1969, voted to resist, its mayor calling the unitary proposal the absorption of the town's ancient rights by the county's rural majority. The port's borough, threatened equally, voted to resist separately, its resolution of 28 July 1969 arriving with its own paper. The rural districts' association, unexpectedly, also demurs, its secretary saying the districts' intimacy was the mosaic's virtue. The government's reception of the report is cautious, a white paper promised for the new year. The commission sat for two hundred and eleven days and heard nine hundred witnesses, the county's clerks among them.
[2] Title: White Paper Cuts the Commission's Coat Differently, Day: February 17, 1971 Content: The government's white paper of 16 February 1971 answers the royal commission with a different geography: not one unitary authority but two, the town and the port to be merged as a single district within a two-tier structure, the county council retained above six districts. The commission's unitary proposal is dropped, the white paper's preface citing the county's evidence for the mosaic's defence — the town's ancient rights and the rural districts' intimacy both quoted. The town's mayor, the resistance of 1969's leader, called the white paper a victory for the boroughs, though the borough itself is to be merged with the port, its rival of a century, the two councils' buildings two hundred yards apart. The port's council, at its meeting of 22 February 1971, voted to accept the merger as the lesser coat, its resolution asking only that the new district's name carry the port's. The bill itself is promised for the autumn session, its provisions to take effect in 1974. The commission's chairman, asked for his view, said that unitary government would come, in a century or a parliament, whichever was longer.
[3] Title: New Councils Take Office, the Port's Name Survives, Day: April 2, 1974 Content: The new structure of local government took office on 1 April 1974: the county council retained, six districts beneath it, the town and the port merged as the town-and-port district with the port's name in the title after all. The first elections to the new councils were held on 7 June 1973, the members elected then taking their seats now for the shadow year's end. The district's first council, forty members meeting in the town's hall with the port's mace carried beside the town's, chose as its first leader a port councillor, the merger's lesser coat now the greater honour. The twenty-one rural districts of the old mosaic are three districts now, their intimacy re-scaled. The county's clerk of 1969, retired since, told the merger's inaugural dinner that the commission's two hundred and eleven days had at least taught the county its own geography. The unitary question, the chairman's century-or-parliament, remains unanswered: the new structure's first test, the rates, falls due in the autumn. The two buildings, two hundred yards apart, are to be one headquarters by 1976.
