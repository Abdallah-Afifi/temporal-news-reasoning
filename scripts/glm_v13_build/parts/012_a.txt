[1] Title: Port Expansion Wins Consent, Day: November 7, 2006 Content: The harbour authority's scheme for a deep-water terminal at Merrow Point received consent on 6 November 2006, after nineteen months of hearings. The scheme — a quarter mile of new quay, a dredged channel to fourteen metres, and the reclamation of thirty acres — is designed for the new generation of container ships too deep for the present locks, which date from 1909. Objectors, led by the sailing club and the shorebird trust, won a realignment of the reclamation and a monitoring station on the spit, but failed to stop the scheme outright. The authority's chairman said construction would begin in the spring of 2007 and first shipping was hoped for 2009. The dredged channel's destiny, the largest single element, is to be consented under a separate instrument still before the marine directorate. The port's pilots, whose numbers have fallen from forty-one to twenty-six since 1997, are to be retrained for the larger vessels. The chamber of shipping called the consent the region's most important since the container itself.
[2] Title: Dredging Begins as Reclamation Rises, Day: April 16, 2008 Content: The dredger fleet began cutting the new channel on 14 April 2008, a year behind the consent's optimistic schedule, the marine directorate's separate instrument having been granted only on 20 September 2007 after the bird-nesting season's surveys. The reclamation, started in the spring of 2007, has reached nineteen of its thirty acres, its geotextile edge visible at low tide. The project's cost has risen from one hundred and eighty to two hundred and ten million pounds, the authority attributing the rise to steel and to the delayed start. The shorebird trust's monitoring station, operating since 1 August 2007, has recorded no exceedances, a finding its own scientists describe as surprising and the sailing club's members describe as convenient. The pilots' retraining, begun in January 2008, has graduated its first eight. The authority still promises first shipping in 2009, though the quays' cranes, ordered in December 2007 from a yard with its own delays, are now spoken of as arriving in the spring of 2010. The contractor has doubled its night shifts on the quay wall.
[3] Title: Terminal Opens a Year Late, Busy From the First Tide, Day: July 13, 2010 Content: The Merrow deep-water terminal opened to shipping on 12 July 2010, thirteen months later than the consent's 2009 hope, the delay inherited entire from the channel's own year of waiting. The first caller, a 9,600-container vessel too large for the old locks, discharged and sailed on the same tide. The terminal's first year saw throughput of four hundred and ten thousand containers, against a business case of three hundred and fifty, and the authority's annual report of 2011 records the channel dredged to its full fourteen metres by September 2010. The cranes that arrived in the spring of 2010 were joined by two more in 2011's budget. The shorebird trust's monitoring station, funded for ten years from the consent, published its five-year report on 30 June 2012 finding the spit's colonies stable. The pilots' complement, twenty-six when the consent was granted, is now thirty-four. The sailing club's realigned racing marks were blessed in a ceremony the commodore called making the best of it. The authority's chairman called the terminal the port's second founding.

[1] Title: Automation Plans Divulged at Terminal, Day: February 21, 2012 Content: The harbour authority confirmed on 20 February 2012 that it is studying full automation of the Merrow container yard — remote cranes, driverless straddle carriers, and a gate staffed by cameras — with a decision to be taken by the board in the autumn. The study, commissioned from the engineering consultancy last summer, puts the jobs at risk at one hundred and sixty of the terminal's four hundred and ten. The authority's chief executive said no decision had been made and none would be without consultation; the union's regional secretary, reached by phone, said the confirmation itself was the decision, and called a shop stewards' meeting for 27 February 2012. The terminal, busy since its opening year of 2010, is running at nine-tenths of its first-phase capacity, which the authority says is the very problem: productivity per worker must rise or the shipping lines will call elsewhere. The pilots, automation-proof so far as anyone can see, kept their own counsel. The chamber of shipping welcomed the study as ordinary modernisation.
[2] Title: Strike Shuts Terminal for Nine Days, Day: May 14, 2013 Content: The strike at Merrow terminal, begun at six on the morning of 6 May 2013 after the board approved the automation plan by nine votes to two on 2 May 2013, ended in the early hours of 15 May 2013 after nine days. The pickets held the gates completely for six days; from the fourth day the authority ran a skeleton shift of staff who crossed, moving perishables only. Eleven vessels diverted to the northern ports, and the shipping lines are said to have levied their contract's delay penalties, a figure the authority will not confirm. The settlement, brokered by the mediation service over the weekend of 11-12 May 2013, provides a guarantee of no compulsory redundancies to the end of 2016, a retraining and wage-protection package, and the union's agreement to the remote cranes — the driverless straddles deferred to a joint review reporting in 2015. Both sides claimed the other blinked. The union's ballot had returned eighty-one per cent for action on 25 April 2013 on a turnout of ninety-two.
[3] Title: Joint Review Defers the Driverless Yard, Day: October 8, 2014 Content: The joint review of the automation programme, reporting on 7 October 2014, has recommended that the driverless straddle carriers be deferred indefinitely, the remote cranes having proved themselves in eighteen months of operation. The review, chaired by the retired arbitrator appointed under the May 2013 settlement, found the straddles' sensor performance wanting in the terminal's own fog, an atmospheric condition the sales brochures had omitted. The no-compulsory-redundancy guarantee, running to the end of 2016, has seen the terminal's complement fall from four hundred and ten to three hundred and eighty-five by attrition and transfer. The diverted shipping of the 2013 strike returned within the year, and the terminal's throughput for 2013 as a whole fell only four per cent. The joint training centre funded under the settlement graduated its hundredth trainee on 30 September 2014. The union's regional secretary, defeated for re-election this spring, told the members the 2013 strike was the making of the branch. The authority's board has accepted the review in full.

[1] Title: Ferry Tender Awarded to Low Bidder, Day: June 12, 2007 Content: the island ferry route's seven-year tender has been awarded to Coastal Lines, the lowest of three bidders, the maritime directorate announced on 11 June 2007. Coastal Lines' offer, eleven per cent below the incumbent's, promises two second-hand ro-ro vessels and a doubling of winter sailings from the 2008 season. The incumbent, which has run the route since privatisation in 1993 and carried 310,000 passengers last year, called the tender's weighting of price over seamanship a mistake the island would come to rue. The island's council, split, welcomed the winter service and wondered about the boats. Coastal Lines' chairman said the second-hand vessels, presently trading in the Baltic, would be refitted through the autumn and winter of 2007-08. The directorate's statement notes the tender's subsidy, previously annual, is fixed for the seven years. The route's user group asked what happened if the subsidy's value were eaten by fuel. The first Coastal sailing is promised for 28 March 2008, the start of the season.
[2] Title: Ferry Operator Collapses Mid-Season, Day: August 25, 2009 Content: Coastal Lines called in administrators on 24 August 2009, two years and a season into its seven-year tender, stranding three hundred and forty passengers on the island and a vessel's worth of freight in the mainland yard. The route's last sailing was the evening of 23 August 2009. The administrators' first statement blamed fuel, the fixed subsidy, and the refit loans of 2007-08; the second-hand vessels are owned by a bank. The maritime directorate invoked its continuity powers within the day, and the incumbent operator — the very company the 2007 tender displaced — was engaged on an emergency contract on 26 August 2009 to run a skeleton service from the morning of 28 August 2009. The island's businesses, at the end of the tourist season, counted the coaches cancelled and the perishables spoiled. The council's leader, who had wondered about the boats in 2007, said the island had its answer. The stranded passengers crossed on the first emergency sailing with their tickets honoured. The user group's chairman said the subsidy's fixed seven years had been a forecast, and forecasts fail.
[3] Title: New Ten Years for the Old Hands, Day: February 16, 2011 Content: The island route's new ten-year contract, awarded without competition to the incumbent under the continuity provisions and signed on 15 February 2011, restores the service's standing and raises the subsidy by a third, index-linked. The incumbent has bought the two second-hand vessels from the bank that owned them, and both, refitted again, were surveyed and passed in January 2011. The winter sailings that Coastal Lines promised in 2007 and ran in 2008 are in the new schedule as of right. The island's passenger numbers, 310,000 in the incumbent's last year of 2007, fell to 265,000 in 2010, the collapse year's autumn and the emergency service's austerity being felt. The directorate's review of the failed tender, published on 10 December 2010, found Coastal Lines' bid arithmetically sound and commercially fantastical, and recommends financial capacity tests of a rigour the 2007 process lacked. The council's leader, at the signing, thanked the crews of the emergency autumn of 2009. The user group asked for a seat in the contract's monitoring, and got one.
