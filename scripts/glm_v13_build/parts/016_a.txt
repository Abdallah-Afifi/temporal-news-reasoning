[1] Title: University Announces Science Quarter, Day: November 17, 1993 Content: The university announced on 16 November 1993 a forty-million-pound science quarter on the south paddocks, to be built in three phases by the year 2000 and to house the chemistry, computing and materials departments now scattered in prefabs from the 1960s. The funding, a third from the funding council's estates initiative and the rest from a bank facility secured on the university's ancient endowment, was agreed by the council on 9 November 1993 after a year of quiet preparation. The vice-chancellor of two years' standing called it the institution's largest building programme since the quadrangle of 1912. The students' union, consulted by memorandum rather than meeting, objected to the paddocks' loss as a recreational field, and was answered with a sports hall in phase two. The architects, a practice of zoo and museum fame, promise buildings that will weather to the colour of the town's stone. Groundbreaking is set for March 1994. The bank's facility, the bursar notes, assumes the prefabs' demolition saves their maintenance from 1996.
[2] Title: Phase One Halved as Costs Bite, Day: June 20, 1995 Content: The science quarter's phase one, groundbreaking'd in March 1994, has been halved in scope by the university council, the decision taken 19 June 1995 and announced with the accounts. The funding council's estates initiative, the third of the money promised in 1993, was cut nationally in the autumn of 1994, and the bank's facility, secured on the endowment, has been drawn to its full forty million with the chemistry building alone to show. The halved phase one — chemistry done, computing deferred to a phase two now undated — means the prefabs of the 1960s stand until further notice, their maintenance saving of 1996 unbudgeted and their tenants unamused. The architects have left, the practice of zoo fame having resigned over the halving in April 1995, and a local firm of school-extension pedigree completes. The students' union's sports hall, promised in phase two, is recast as a permanent deferral, the paddocks' remaining half to serve as the field it always was. The vice-chancellor of 1991's standing survives the council's vote, nine to seven with abstentions.
[3] Title: Quarter Finished at Last, Prefabs Fall, Day: October 9, 2000 Content: The science quarter's final building, computing at last, was opened on 6 October 2000 by the chancellor, the programme begun in 1994 finishing in the year its 1993 announcement had promised, if with nothing else intact. The story of the money — the estates initiative's third cut in 1994, the bank facility drawn full by 1995 — was answered by the endowment's quiet land sales of 1997-98, three meadows to the town's developers, and by the alumni appeal of 1998 which raised eleven million against a target of six. The architects of zoo fame, resigned 1995, were re-engaged in 1998 for the computing building alone, their weathering stone now on all four. The prefabs of the 1960s, whose fall the bursar had budgeted for 1996, came down in the summer of 1999, their last tenants moving to the new chemistry building's empty third floor. The students' union's sports hall, promised in 1993's phase two and deferred in 1995, was opened on 12 September 2000, the union's current president calling it a victory of asking for seven years. The vice-chancellor retired in July, the quarter his epitaph.

[1] Title: Cold Fusion Claim Stuns Colleagues, Day: March 24, 1999 Content: The institute's materials group, led by the professor whose cold-chemistry work has long divided the field, announced on 23 March 1999 a tabletop experiment yielding excess heat at room temperature, the results posted to the institute's preprint server at six in the evening. The claim, if it holds, rewrites the energy textbooks; the professor, careful in person, calls it a phenomenon first and an explanation later. The apparatus — palladium electrodes and a bath of heavy water — cost eleven thousand pounds. The preprint's data run to forty pages, the excess heat charted over eleven days from 3 March to 14 March 1999, the trace rising after the fifth day and holding. Two national laboratories telephoned the institute before noon on the 24th seeking the electrode supplier. The institute's director, cabinet-ministerial in caution, has called a symposium for 12 May 1999 and asked the group to attempt a public demonstration. The field's sceptics, burned by the claims of 1989, ask for the electrodes' calibration history; the group replies that the calorimetry is the paper's appendix.
[2] Title: Replication Fails as Doubts Gather, Day: February 15, 2001 Content: The institute's cold-chemistry group's excess-heat phenomenon has failed replication at the two national laboratories that first telephoned, their reports, both dated 31 January 2001, finding no anomaly beyond the calorimetry's own errors. The public demonstration demanded in 1999 and given on 12 May 1999 to a hall of four hundred, produced a trace the group's own instrument now cannot reproduce — the group attributes this to the electrodes' batch, the laboratories to the group's calibration history. The institute's internal review, commissioned in June 2000 and reporting on 9 February 2001, finds no misconduct but a record-keeping lapse: the original electrodes of March 1999 were discarded in the autumn of that year, the calorimetry's logs incomplete for days six through nine. The symposium of May 1999 is remembered as the field's last crowded hour; the two national laboratories have closed their replication programmes. The professor, careful still, concedes the phenomenon's burden of proof has returned to his bench. The preprint of March 1999 has been cited four hundred times, half the citations sceptical.
[3] Title: Phenomenon Traced to Contamination at Last, Day: November 26, 2002 Content: The cold-chemistry group's own final paper, published 25 November 2002, closes the episode: the excess heat of March 1999 was traced to a contaminated heavy-water batch, its deuterium enriched beyond specification by a supplier's faulty valve. The group's two-year re-examination, funded by a skeptic's foundation on terms permitting publication either way, re-ran the original protocol with logged electrodes and fresh batches, and found nothing but chemistry's ordinary ledger. The professor, who conceded the burden in 2001, accepts the finding in the paper's last paragraph and thanks the foundation's founder, his sharpest critic, for the money that settled it. The record-keeping lapse of the 2001 review — electrodes discarded, logs incomplete — is addressed in the paper's methods, the group's new logging regime now the institute's standard. The citations of the 1999 preprint have turned wholly historical. The contaminated batch's supplier, traced through the valve's serial, settled out of court in October 2002. The institute's director, cabinet-ministerial to the end, calls the affair science working, slowly.

[1] Title: County Votes to Close Village Schools, Day: November 6, 2001 Content: The county council voted on 5 November 2001 to consult on closing eleven village primary schools, the roll numbers having fallen by a third since 1995 across the district's rural half. The cabinet member's report, published 2 November 2001, names the eleven and promises the pupils carriage to the market-town federations from September 2003. The eleven, some with rolls under twenty, cost the county three times the per-pupil average of the federations. The villages' first response was the meeting in the largest threatened school's hall on 9 November 2001, four hundred strong, and the formation of an action alliance by the 12th. The teachers' unions are split, the rural members' jobs against the urban members' class sizes. The diocese, proprietor of four of the eleven through their church foundation, has signalled resistance through the courts if need be. The consultation runs to 15 February 2002. The county's leader says the numbers, not the will, drive the plan, and the numbers are the children's absence.
[2] Title: Consultation Ends, Seven Schools Saved, Day: March 19, 2002 Content: The county's consultation, closed 15 February 2002 with two thousand three hundred responses, ended on 19 March 2002 in a partial climbdown: seven of the eleven schools are reprieved, four to close in August 2003. The reprieved seven's fate turns on federation — shared head teachers, shared specialists, the market town's sixth formers bussed out to teach the village infants their French — a model the action alliance itself proposed in January. The diocese's four church schools all stand reprieved, the courts having been mentioned and the county having blinked, the alliance's newsletter says. The four closing schools, all county foundation, have rolls of fourteen, seventeen, nineteen and nine. Their pupils' carriage is contracted from September 2003, the journeys averaging forty minutes. The alliance's chairman, a farmer's wife with four children through the largest reprieved school, told the county's cabinet that seven was a defeat with honours. The leader replied that the numbers had moved: two of the seven had found housing-estate children the 2001 report had missed.
[3] Title: Federated Schools' First Year Judged, Day: November 15, 2004 Content: The federated village schools' first full year, 2003-04, is judged a success in the county's review published 12 November 2004: the seven reprieved schools' rolls have risen by nine per cent, the housing estates' children of the 2002 report now arrived in person. The four closed schools' buildings pass to community trusts, the first of their conveyances dated 30 June 2004, and two of the four now house the post office and the playgroup, the classrooms' maps left on the walls. The federation's shared head, appointed 1 September 2003 across three villages, drives the eleven miles between them and says the model stands or falls on the driving. The market-town sixth formers' French teaching, the alliance's own proposal, has become the county's flagship, praised in the inspection report of May 2004. The alliance, its fight won to seven, dissolved itself at the annual meeting of 20 April 2004, its chairman taking the county's education chair the next year. The carriages of the closed four average forty-one minutes still, the county's small honesty.
