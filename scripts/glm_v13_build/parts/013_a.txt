[1] Title: Roadworks Uncover Roman Fort Ditch, Day: August 12, 1967 Content: Contractors cutting the bypass alignment through Harker Field struck a wide dark band on 10 August 1967, and the county archaeologist called in on the 11th confirmed it as the ditch of a previously unrecorded Roman fort. The road scheme, consented in 1965 and begun this spring, is stopped over two hundred yards while a rescue excavation is mounted. The Ministry has granted three weeks and eight hundred pounds. A squad of students and volunteers, led by the archaeologist and a lecturer from the university, began work on 14 August 1967 with borrowed wheelbarrows and a hired mechanical excavator. Finds on the first days include samian ware, a bronze brooch, and a coin of Vespasian, giving a first-century date. The ditches' plan, traced by Sunday 20 August 1967, suggests an occupation of a decade or two, not a permanent camp. The road engineers have offered a realignment costing an extra forty thousand pounds, which the county says it cannot find.
[2] Title: Dig Extended as Road Waits, Day: September 5, 1967 Content: The rescue excavation at Harker Field was extended by four weeks on 4 September 1967, the Ministry doubling its grant and the road contractors swallowing a month of standing time. The dig has moved from the ditches to the interior: timber building slots, a bread oven, and a cobbled street have emerged, and the coin list now runs from Vespasian to Hadrian, seventy-one coins in all. The volunteers' numbers grew to sixty at the August bank holiday and have held. A second-century inscription fragment, the dig's prize, was found on 29 August 1967 and removed to the county museum the same evening for safekeeping. The university's lecturer dates the fort's abandonment to the 120s, the coin sequence stopping short of Antoninus Pius. The county has found the realignment money after all — the extra forty thousand pounds was voted on 1 September 1967 — and the road will now swing south of the whole site, touching nothing.
[3] Title: Fort Site Scheduled as Road Opens, Day: July 8, 1968 Content: The Harker fort site was entered on the schedule of ancient monuments on 5 July 1968, and the bypass that occasioned its discovery opened to traffic on 6 July 1968 along its realigned course south of the ditches. The excavation's report, published in the county journal on 2 July 1968, runs to two hundred pages and four hundred figures; its verdict is a fort of the 70s-120s, built under the first governors and abandoned quietly, the garrison moving east. The finds — the Vespasianic coin of the first day, the inscription, the brooch — went on public display at the county museum on 20 July 1968 in a case funded by the road contractors as a peace offering. The students of the dig founded an archaeological society that same autumn, its first lecture given by the county archaeologist on 15 October 1968 to a hall of three hundred. The Ministry's grants, three weeks' eight hundred pounds grown to eight weeks' four thousand, are cited in parliament as rescue archaeology's proving ground.

[1] Title: Museum Roof Fails in Night Storm, Day: January 18, 1979 Content: The west roof of the county museum collapsed under snow in the early hours of 17 January 1979, and the water that followed the collapse reached the archaeology gallery's lower cases. The gallery was closed on 18 January 1979 and the first salvage began the same day: the Harker inscription fragment and the coin hoards were crated and moved to the strongroom by noon, dry and intact. The Victorian natural history cases were less lucky, and the taxidermy is written off. The museum's insurer sent assessors within the week. The director's statement thanks the fire brigade, whose pumps ran from three in the morning, and the volunteers who formed a human chain in the reading room. The building, of 1888, has been condemned as far as the west wing is concerned; the east wing, drier and sound, will reopen as soon as the boilers are certified. The county's emergency committee met on 19 January 1979 and voted fifty thousand pounds for stabilization. A longer question — rebuild or replace — was put off for a report in the spring.
[2] Title: Museum to Rebuild Wing on Old Footprint, Day: May 15, 1979 Content: The county's emergency committee, on the strength of the spring report delivered 10 May 1979, voted on 14 May 1979 to rebuild the museum's west wing on its Victorian footprint, at a cost of four hundred and twenty thousand pounds. The report had weighed replacement on a new site and found it dearer by half and worse for the town. The insurance settlement, agreed on 30 April 1979 at one hundred and eighty thousand pounds, will offset the salvage and storage; the rest comes from the rates over five years. The archaeology gallery's contents, crated since January, are to tour the region's schools in the interim — the Harker finds' first tour, beginning 11 June 1979 with the inscription fragment behind school-stage glass. The rebuilding contract was let on 20 July 1979 to the firm whose realignment saved Harker Field in 1967, a symmetry the press enjoyed. Completion is promised for the spring of 1982. The boilers of the east wing were certified on 2 April 1979 and the wing reopened quietly the next day.
[3] Title: Gallery Reopens Three Years On, Day: April 20, 1982 Content: The rebuilt archaeology gallery opened to the public on 19 April 1982, thirty-eight months after the roof fell and a month ahead of the contract's promise. The Harker finds returned from their schools' tour on 15 March 1982 and were reinstalled in a case where the inscription is lit from below, a design the director credits to a schoolboy's suggestion made during the tour. Attendance on the first week ran at three times the old gallery's average, and the museum's friends' society, founded in the salvage winter of 1979, took its thousandth member on 24 April 1982. The rebuilding's final account, published with the opening, came in at four hundred and thirty-one thousand pounds, eleven thousand over the 1979 vote. The emergency committee's minutes of the decision, May 1979, are displayed in the new wing's lobby at the county recorder's insistence. The tour's log book — ninety-one schools in three years — is exhibited beside it. The 1888 east wing, closed each winter since the collapse for economy, will now stay open, the county says, the new wing's receipts paying the difference.

[1] Title: Saxon Barrow Threatened by Ploughing, Day: March 22, 1974 Content: The round barrow on Challis Hill, the last of seven that crowned the ridge in the last century's maps, is to be ploughed out this spring, its farmer-owner having given notice under the field monuments act. The barrow, unexcavated and scheduled in 1936, stands in the way of irrigation mains for the new root-crop regime. The county archaeologist inspected on 21 March 1974 and reports the mound sound, though the plough has been nibbling its foot for twenty years. The ministry's inspector has visited twice; the ancient monuments board sits on the matter in April. The farmer, third of his line to work the hill, says he has refused the barrow's destruction once before, in 1958, for his father's sake, and will not refuse again for a subsidy's. A rescue excavation, if the board permits destruction, would be the barrow's first opening since the Saxons heaped it. The local society has begun a petition, two hundred names by Sunday, asking for the mound's purchase. The water table, the farmer observes, has fallen since his grandfather's day, and the barrow with it.
[2] Title: Barrow Opened as Board Gives Way, Day: July 30, 1974 Content: The ancient monuments board, sitting on 26 April 1974, refused both destruction and purchase, and ordered instead a full excavation with the farmer compensated for the land's loss — a formula none had proposed and all accepted. The dig began on 1 July 1974 and ended on 26 July 1974, twenty-five days in the field. The barrow proved to be of the seventh century, its primary burial a warrior with shield, spear, and a gold-foil cross, the conversion generation's emblem. A secondary burial, inserted at the mound's edge a century later, was a woman with a workbox and beads. The petition's two hundred names, the local society's doing, are credited by the county archaeologist with stiffening the board. The farmer took his compensation on 15 May 1974 and, at the dig's end, the finds' first display in his barn, two hundred neighbours filing past the tables. The gold-foil cross went to the county museum under treasury grant on 20 July 1974; the rest of the assemblage follows when conserved.
[3] Title: Barrow Finds Go Home to a New Museum, Day: June 11, 1988 Content: The Challis Hill assemblage — warrior, woman, cross, and workbox — returned to within sight of the barrow's ghost on 10 June 1988, opening in the district's new heritage centre, built at the crossroads with a county grant and a local appeal. The cross, stored at the county museum since 1974, heads the display; the woman's beads, conserved over fourteen years, lie in a drawer the visitor may open. The farmer whose notice began it all, seventy-one now, cut the ribbon and said the hill had settled its account. The 1958 refusal, his father's barrow and his own, is told in the display's last case, the petition of 1974 beside it in facsimile. The heritage centre's appeal raised ninety-four thousand pounds in fourteen months from a district of eleven thousand souls, a figure the county recorder calls the district's record. The excavator's 1974 report, long promised, is published this month in the county journal with the full grave plans. The centre's first season's attendance is forecast at thirty thousand, the barrow's field a car park in August when the bridle path reopens.
