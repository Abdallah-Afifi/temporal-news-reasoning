[1] Title: Power Station Conversion Announced, Day: March 12, 1994 Content: The generating board announced on 11 March 1994 that the Corrin power station will be converted from coal to dual firing with natural gas, securing its future into the next century. The conversion, to be completed by the autumn of 1997, will cut the station's sulphur emissions by four-fifths and cost one hundred and ten million pounds. The station's coal supply contract with the Trelech complex, running since 1968, will be halved rather than ended, preserving an estimated two hundred mining jobs. Local authorities reacted warmly, the chamber of trade cautiously, and the miners' union with reserved judgement pending the detail. The gas pipeline spur, nine kilometres from the trunk line, will require its own consents and is expected to take a year. The board's chairman said the conversion answers both the new air quality rules and the station's aging boilers, forty of which date from the original 1966 commissioning. No layoffs are planned among the station's three hundred and ten workers, though retraining will be extensive.
[2] Title: Gas Arrives at Corrin Ahead of Schedule, Day: June 21, 1997 Content: Gas was first burned at Corrin station on 14 June 1997, twelve weeks ahead of the conversion schedule, and the final coal-only boiler was retired the same month. The conversion's official completion ceremony has been fixed for 30 September 1997, when the first minister will switch the station to gas-primary firing. The pipeline spur was pressure-tested in April and accepted in May, and the sulphur monitors on the town's roofs have already recorded a fall. The halved coal contract has been renegotiated again, the Trelech complex guaranteeing low-sulphur blends for the remaining boilers. The station's retraining programme finished its last cohort on 6 June 1997, with two hundred and eighty workers certified on gas plant. The board confirms the project came in eight million pounds under budget. The miners' union, which had reserved judgement in 1994, has welcomed the jobs preserved and noted the pit's own future remains open beyond 2000.
[3] Title: Coal Contract Ends as Corrin Goes Gas-Primary, Day: February 10, 2000 Content: The generating board confirmed on 9 February 2000 that the residual coal contract with the Trelech complex will lapse at the end of June 2000, ending thirty-two years of coal supply to Corrin station. The station has burned gas-primary since the conversion completed in 1997, with coal held as standby only; the standby is now judged redundant given the grid's margin. Trelech's owners said two hundred and sixty jobs are affected at the pit, of which a hundred transfers to the maintenance arm are offered. The board's statement pays tribute to the workforce and cites the 1994 conversion decision as the station's salvation. Emissions figures published with the statement show sulphur down eighty-two per cent against the 1993 baseline. The town's roof monitors, retained since the conversion, will be decommissioned in the spring. A last symbolic coal train is planned for 30 June 2000, its wagons already sold to a heritage railway.

[1] Title: Wind Farm Application Submitted for Rannoch Moor, Day: October 8, 1998 Content: The application for a forty-turbine wind farm on Rannoch Moor was lodged on 7 October 1998, the largest such scheme the county has considered. The developer, a consortium of the regional utility and a Danish manufacturer, claims the farm would power sixty thousand homes and save the emission of two hundred thousand tonnes of carbon dioxide a year. Objections were lodged within days by the ramblers' association, the bird club, and three parish councils, citing visibility from the ridge path and the moor's nesting merlins. The planning file will close for representations on 20 November 1998, with a decision expected in the new year. The utility's own surveys, running since 1996, put the moor's wind speeds among the region's best. The tourism board has split, some members fearing the sightlines, others marketing the turbines as an attraction. The developer has offered a community fund of forty thousand pounds a year, an offer the parishes have collectively declined to discuss until the decision.
[2] Title: Rannoch Wind Farm Approved with Conditions, Day: March 19, 1999 Content: The wind farm was approved on 18 March 1999, with fifty-two conditions attached, among them a breeding-season construction ban, a five-metre reduction in blade tip height, and a fifteen-year decommissioning bond. The planning committee's vote was eleven to four after two nights of hearings. The ramblers' association has withdrawn its objection under a condition moving two turbines off the ridge sightline, but the bird club has signalled an appeal to the national authority, calling the merlin surveys inadequate. The developer welcomed the decision and said construction could begin in the autumn, with first power in the summer of 2001. The community fund was raised to sixty thousand pounds a year, index-linked, and this time the parishes accepted, appointing trustees at a joint meeting on 30 March 1999. The conditions bind any sale of the farm, a point the county's lawyers insisted upon after the utility's recent divestments. Grid connection works are consented separately and are not yet before the committee.
[3] Title: First Power from Rannoch as Appeal Fails, Day: August 7, 2001 Content: The Rannoch wind farm fed its first power to the grid on 3 August 2001, fourteen months later than the developer's 1999 projection, the delay caused by access-road appeals and one boggy winter. The bird club's judicial challenge ended on 12 December 2000 when the national authority upheld the approval, the court awarding costs against the club. Twenty-nine of the forty turbines are turning; the rest follow in September. The farm's output in its first week met the forecast for sixty thousand homes. The community fund's first grants were paid on 15 May 2001 to the village halls, and the trustees report satisfaction with the index-linking. The ramblers' sightline condition was audited on 21 June 2001 and found honoured. The developer has applied to repower the access track, a minor consent, and says the decommissioning bond of 1999 has been renewed at the same figure, a point the county is verifying. A visitor car park opened on 1 July 2001 and is fuller than the tourism board predicted.

[1] Title: Blackout Hits Eastern Grid in Winter Storm, Day: January 29, 1993 Content: A fault at the Corrigan switching station cut power to a hundred and ten thousand homes on the evening of 28 January 1993, as the winter storm's winds peaked. Supply was restored to the last districts only on 31 January 1993, after ninety-one hours of rolling reconnections. The fault, a protection relay that failed to trip correctly, is under investigation by the board and by the inspectorate. Hospitals ran on standby plant throughout, and the water works lost pressure in the high villages. The board's chairman apologised on the radio and promised compensation under the guaranteed standards, the first mass invocation of the scheme since its 1990 introduction. Opposition members demanded the resignation of the operations director; the minister demanded instead a report in six weeks. The storm itself, the deepest of the winter, brought down lines across the region, and the board's repair gangs worked double shifts from the first night, aided by two contractors' crews from the neighbouring utility.
[2] Title: Blackout Report Finds Maintenance Lapse, Day: April 10, 1993 Content: The inspectorate's report on the January blackout, published on 9 April 1993, finds that the protection relay at Corrigan had missed its scheduled 1992 test, the maintenance crew having been diverted to storm repairs that autumn. The report criticises the board's scheduling but concludes the fault itself was a rare manufacturing defect that a test might not have caught. Compensation payments under the guaranteed standards reached two hundred and fourteen thousand pounds, paid to seventy-one thousand claimants by 26 March 1993. The operations director survived the calls for his resignation, receiving instead a written warning recorded on 30 March 1993. The board has adopted the report's central recommendation: protection schemes to be tested to a fixed calendar regardless of other calls on crews, the first such audit due on 1 September 1993. The minister told the house the episode revealed a system that failed safely, if expensively. The relay's manufacturer has accepted a joint examination of the defect, to report by the summer.
[3] Title: Grid Reborn: Eastern Loop Completed, Day: November 5, 1994 Content: The eastern loop of the transmission grid was energised on 4 November 1994, closing a ring around the region that the board says would have confined the January 1993 blackout to a fraction of its extent. The loop, forty-one miles of towers consented in 1991 and built through two winters, cost the region's billpayers a levy of eleven pence a quarter. The switching stations at either end were commissioned in September and October, and the Corrigan relay — replaced the day after the blackout — now sits inside a fully audited protection scheme, its test calendar honoured since the September 1993 audit began. The blackout's compensation scheme closed on 31 December 1993 with a final tally of seventy-four thousand claimants. The board's chairman called the loop the region's answer to its hardest night, and the operations director, warning received and career resumed, cut the ribbon. The manufacturers' joint examination of the defective relay reported in July 1994 with a design change, since incorporated in the new installations.
