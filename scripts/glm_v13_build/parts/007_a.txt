[1] Title: Ashcombe Will Goes to Probate, Day: March 4, 1897 Content: The will of the late linen merchant Silas Ashcombe was lodged for probate at the district registry on Monday, opening a suit that solicitors expect to run for years. The instrument itself bears the date 21 May 1895 and divides an estate sworn at forty-eight thousand pounds among three heirs. A codicil signed on 3 December 1896, barely ten weeks before the merchant's death, redirects the share of the younger heir, Walter Ashcombe, and its validity is already questioned by his counsel. The registry listing shows the estate includes the Fennwick Street warehouse, two milling concerns in the county, and a portfolio of colonial debentures. Counsel for the elder daughter, Mrs. Cordelia Voss, indicated that she will contend the codicil was obtained when the testator was of failing mind. The matter stands for first directions before Judge of the County Probate Court later this month. Courtroom regulars recalled no contested estate of this size in the district since the Harborough rope-works litigation of the previous decade, which itself dragged through four years of pleadings before a compromise was signed.
[2] Title: Codicil Upheld in Ashcombe Appeal, Day: June 21, 1898 Content: The appellate division has upheld the disputed codicil to the Ashcombe will by a two-to-one majority, closing the second round of the celebrated probate suit. The appeal hearing opened on 9 May 1898 before three judges sitting in banc, and judgment was delivered on 21 June 1898 after a fortnight of reserved consideration. The majority found that the testator, though frail, retained capacity when he signed the codicil on 3 December 1896, and that the attesting witnesses, including the family solicitor, gave consistent accounts of the signing. Costs of the appeal were ordered against the younger heir, Walter Ashcombe, whose counsel had pressed the claim of undue influence hardest. The dissenting judge would have ordered a new trial on the ground that the medical evidence of December 1896 was never properly before the court. Notice of a possible further application to the higher court was given from the bar, but solicitors for Mrs. Voss indicated their client intends to abide by the ruling rather than prolong the litigation.
[3] Title: Ashcombe Estate Settled at Last, Day: October 14, 1899 Content: Final distribution of the Ashcombe estate was completed on 14 October 1899, thirty months after the will first went to probate in March 1897. The scheme of partition, lodged in July, transfers the Fennwick Street warehouse and the larger milling concern to Mrs. Cordelia Voss under the codicil upheld on appeal in June 1898, while the smaller mill goes to a nephew, Thomas Ashcombe, a baker of Mill Lane. Walter Ashcombe, the younger son defeated in the courts, filed for bankruptcy on 2 August 1899, his schedule showing liabilities of eleven hundred pounds contracted chiefly in trade credit; the bankruptcy is unconnected with the costs order of the appeal, which remains unsatisfied. A trust of four thousand pounds for the district nursing association takes effect under the residuary clause. The family solicitor confirmed that no further proceedings are contemplated, and the registry file was closed on the day of distribution. Thus ends a suit that began with a codicil signed ten weeks before death and outlived two attempts to unsettle it.

[1] Title: Two Dead at Garnett Mill, Day: November 2, 1906 Content: A carding engine at Garnett's Victoria Mill was the scene of a fatal accident on the night shift of 1 November 1906, when two piecers, Ellen Dyson and her cousin Joseph, were caught by an unguarded shaft. The coroner's inquest opened on 6 November 1906 at the Weavers' Arms and heard that the guard had been removed in September 1906 to speed the cleaning of the compound, and had never been refitted. The works manager conceded under questioning that the removal was known to the overlooker, though he denied it was authorised in writing. The factory inspector's representative told the jury that the accident was one of four in the district attributable to unguarded plant that autumn. A verdict of accidental death, with a rider censuring the firm, was returned after two hours, and the coroner undertook to transmit the depositions to the prosecuting authorities. The funeral of the two piecers took place on Sunday, attended by some six hundred operatives, and the mill observed a half-day of silence on the following Tuesday as a mark of respect.
[2] Title: Garnett Mill Fined Over Guard Removal, Day: February 13, 1907 Content: Garnett and Sons, cotton spinners, were fined one hundred and fifty pounds at the borough magistrates' court on 13 February 1907 for removing a guard from machinery contrary to the statute. The firm, through its counsel, pleaded guilty on 11 February 1907, the second day of the hearing, after the overlooker's note book was produced showing the order for the guard's removal in September 1906. The stipendiary magistrate remarked that the penalty would have been heavier had the plea not been tendered before the witnesses were called, and ordered the firm to refit guards throughout the carding room at its own expense within the month. Solicitors for the operatives' union, which had brought the accident to the inspector's notice, said the fine was the largest imposed in the borough for a factory offence in twelve years. The company's cashier paid the sum into court the same afternoon. No order was sought against the overlooker personally, the magistrate taking the view that he acted under instructions.
[3] Title: Quarter Sessions Quashes Mill Conviction, Day: April 28, 1908 Content: The conviction of Garnett and Sons for the Victoria Mill guard offence was quashed on appeal at the quarter sessions on 28 April 1908, the appellate bench holding that the note book entry did not identify the machinery with sufficient certainty. The court heard that the entry of September 1906 referred only to "the compound guard", and that three engines in the carding room answered that description. The recorder allowed the appeal with costs, expressing no opinion on the firm's general practice, and the fine ordered in February 1907 falls to be repaid. It emerged in evidence that a sister mill in the same ownership had suffered an accident of a like kind in 1903, before any of the present proceedings, though the earlier occurrence had not then been prosecuted. The factory inspector's office declined to comment on whether fresh information would be laid. The operatives' union has called a meeting for Sunday next to consider the decision, and the coroner's rider of November 1906 now stands as the only formal censure of the firm on record.

[1] Title: Bremner Arrested Over Leading Article, Day: September 17, 1919 Content: Edmund Bremner, editor of the Border Herald, was arrested at his lodgings on the evening of 16 September 1919 and conveyed to the central police station, where he was charged with publishing seditious libel. The information cites the leading article that appeared in the issue of 9 September 1919, which commented on the coal stoppage and the government's use of emergency regulations. The composing room was entered by warrant the same evening and the formes of the offending issue were seized, together with the paper's file copies. Bremner was released on recognizances of two hundred pounds found by two sureties, and his committal hearing stands fixed for the coming sessions. The Herald's printers, a partnership of long standing, have continued publication of the paper from a jobbing office in the next street, the seized formes notwithstanding. Counsel for the defence has announced that the article is a fair comment on a matter of public concern and that its authorship, and the responsibility for publication, will be contested at every stage. The last prosecution of an editor in the district followed the dock strikes of the previous decade and ended in a withdrawn information.
[2] Title: Bremner Guilty, Sentence Deferred, Day: March 5, 1920 Content: The jury in the Bremner sedition trial returned a verdict of guilty on 4 March 1920, after retiring for slightly more than an hour at the end of a trial that had run five days. Sentence was deferred by the presiding judge to 19 March 1920, when Bremner was sentenced to eighteen months' imprisonment with hard labour, the maximum the court thought fit to impose short of the full statutory term. The co-defendant, a compositor named Auld, was acquitted on the same verdict, the jury accepting that he had set the type without knowledge of its purport. Notice of appeal was given in open court immediately after sentence, and the appeal stands returnable in the summer sittings. Petitions for remission are in circulation among the printing trades, and the Herald's circulation, so its printers report, has doubled since the arrest of the editor last autumn. The court declined to state a case on the fairness of the comment, holding that the question was one of fact for the jury alone.
[3] Title: Privy Council Cuts Bremner Term, Day: July 30, 1921 Content: The judicial committee of the Privy Council has allowed Bremner's appeal in part, reducing the sentence of eighteen months passed in March 1920 to the term already served. The appeal was heard on 12 May 1921 and judgment was delivered on 30 July 1921, the committee holding that the hard-labour component could not stand on the facts found by the jury. Bremner, who has been in custody since a bail variation of February last, was released on 2 August 1921, walking out of the prison gates to a crowd of some hundreds of well-wishers. His paper, the Border Herald, resumed full publication from its own offices the following morning under an editorship reconstituted during his imprisonment. The seized formes, taken in September 1919, remain in the custody of the court pending an application for their return, no date for which has been fixed. Costs of the appeal were not asked for and none were granted. The compositor Auld, acquitted at trial, has since left the district, and no proceedings of any kind are pending against him.
