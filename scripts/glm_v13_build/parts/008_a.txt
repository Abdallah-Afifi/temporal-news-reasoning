[1] Title: Storm Vesper Batters the Coast, Day: December 7, 2016 Content: Storm Vesper made landfall on the night of 5 December 2016, driving a surge that overtopped the harbour wall at Saltrow and flooded one hundred and six homes in the eastern streets. The tide gauge at the pier recorded its highest reading since the gauge was installed in 1961, and the coastguard closed the promenade from the morning of 6 December 2016 until the weekend. Two schools served as rest centres, and the electricity board switched power to the eastern grid as a precaution. The flood warden scheme, volunteers with sirens and lists, was credited by the council with warning most households an hour before the water arrived. The storm's winds also brought down the last mature poplars of the promenade, planted in 1953. Beach huts were swept a quarter mile along the sand. The council leader promised a full review of the defences within the year, and the first minister offered military assistance with the clear-up, which the council accepted for the weekend only.
[2] Title: Flood Grants Open for Saltrow Homes, Day: February 15, 2017 Content: The recovery grant scheme for homes flooded by Storm Vesper opened on 13 February 2017, ten weeks after the storm itself. The scheme, administered by the district council with national funding, offers up to five thousand pounds per household for drying, replastering, and flood-resistant fittings. By the opening date, four hundred and ten applications had been pre-registered, and the council has hired six temporary caseworkers. The caravan park on the dunes, where forty families lived through the winter, reports that all but eleven homes are now reoccupied. Merchants on the harbour front say trade is at four-fifths of the pre-storm level. The council's flood warden scheme is recruiting a second cohort, its numbers having been depleted by the very event it was praised for surviving. A memorial service for the two coastguard reservists injured in the night's work — both now recovered — was held on 5 February 2017 at the harbour church. The grant scheme closes at the end of March.
[3] Title: Barrier Approved After Vesper Review, Day: June 20, 2018 Content: The review of the Saltrow defences, commissioned the day after Storm Vesper and reported on 9 October 2017, has borne fruit: the environment ministry announced on 18 June 2018 that a tidal barrier at the harbour mouth is approved in principle. The review had found the existing wall, raised in 1987, adequate only to a one-in-thirty-year event, and the new barrier is designed for one-in-two-hundred. Construction is projected to begin in 2021 and last four years, with the promenade rebuilt in its shadow. The announcement was welcomed by the residents' association, which had gathered four thousand signatures, and by the chamber of trade, which fears the disruption of the works less than the repetition of the flood. The council notes that the grant scheme of February 2017 closed with six hundred and two households assisted. The poplar promenade is to be replanted with salt-tolerant species in the autumn. No decision has been taken on the barrier's operating regime, the gates' closure rules to be settled at the design stage.

[1] Title: Heatwave Breaks All Records, Day: July 27, 2018 Content: The district's weather station recorded 35.7 degrees on 26 July 2018, the highest reading in a series running back to 1904, beating the previous mark set in August 2003. The heatwave began in earnest on 8 July 2018 and has scarcely relented since, with nineteen consecutive days above twenty-eight degrees. Rail operators imposed speed restrictions from 23 July 2018 after a buckled rail near the viaduct, and the hospital reported a doubling of admissions for dehydration among the elderly. The moors are tinder-dry, and the fire service has camped a crew by the ridge since 19 July 2018. Reservoir levels stand at fifty-two per cent of capacity, against a July average of eighty. Water companies have urged restraint, and the angling club has suspended fishing on the lower river, where flows are at their lowest in living memory. Meteorologists attribute the pattern to a stalled high pressure system, and offer no firm end date.
[2] Title: Hosepipe Ban Announced as Reservoirs Fall, Day: August 10, 2018 Content: The water company announced a hosepipe ban on 9 August 2018, the first in the region since 1976, to take effect from 13 August 2018. Reservoir levels have fallen to forty-four per cent, and the company's drought plan, last rehearsed on paper in 2012, has moved to its second stage. The ban covers garden watering and car washing, with fines of up to one thousand pounds, though the company says enforcement will begin with warnings. Farmers' irrigation licences were curtailed on 1 August 2018, and the potato harvest is expected early and small. The company is applying for a drought permit to take more from the river, a step opposed by the anglers and by the environment agency's local officers. Night-time leak repair crews have doubled. The company's managing director apologised for the timing of the announcement, which coincided with the bank holiday, and promised standpipes would not be needed this year.
[3] Title: Reservoirs Recover After Autumn Rain, Day: January 15, 2019 Content: The rains of autumn 2018 broke the drought: reservoir levels, which touched a low of thirty-nine per cent in early September 2018, had recovered to ninety-one per cent by 4 January 2019, and the hosepipe ban was lifted on 2 December 2018. The water company's final drought report, published on 10 December 2018, records twenty-two thousand warnings issued under the ban and no fines. The farmers' compensation scheme, administered by the rural board, paid out on claims lodged between 20 August 2018 and 30 November 2018, covering irrigation shortfalls and crop losses; the board's chairman said the potato yield came in at sixty per cent of the five-year average. The buckled-rail incident of 23 July 2018 is cited in the rail operator's new heat protocol, which sets action levels from thirty-two degrees. The fire service's ridge camp, stood down on 30 August 2018, dealt with forty-one moorland blazes in the season, against an average of nine. The angling club reopened the lower river on 1 December 2018.

[1] Title: Moorland Fires Rage for a Third Week, Day: July 2, 2019 Content: The wildfires on Blackstone Edge have burned since 15 June 2019, and the fire service now describes them as the largest moorland fires in the county's recorded history. Some nine square kilometres of blanket bog are alight or burnt, and the smoke plume was visible from forty miles on 25 June 2019. Fifty firefighters and two helicopters have been committed, with a specialist heather-burning team brought in from the north on 28 June 2019. The cause is thought to be a campfire, though investigators have not ruled out glass in the sun. The nesting season has been devastated, and the wildlife trust reports the loss of two scheduled breeding sites. Water abstraction for firefighting has lowered the chain of reservoirs by four per cent. The peat, dry after an arid spring, burns below the surface, and the service expects the smouldering to outlast the flames by weeks. Residents with breathing difficulties were advised indoors from 24 June 2019.
[2] Title: Edge Villages Breathe Again as Rains Help, Day: July 24, 2019 Content: Rain on 21 July 2019 did what three weeks of firefighting could not, and by 23 July 2019 the fire service reduced the Edge fires to monitoring status. The two villages under the smoke plume have reopened their summer fêtes, postponed from the first weekend of July. The public health director's analysis, published on 19 July 2019, links the smoke to a thirty per cent rise in inhaler prescriptions and eighty-one extra GP visits, but no deaths. The fire service's demobilisation began on 23 July 2019, though a crew will walk the burnt ground daily for a further fortnight, watching for reignition in the peat. The helicopters were released on 22 July 2019. Costings circulated to the authority put the suppression cost at two million pounds. The campfire theory is now the official line, and the national park's camping byelaws are to be reviewed in the autumn.
[3] Title: Replanting Plan for the Burnt Edge, Day: October 8, 2019 Content: The partnership of wildlife trust, water company, and national park published its restoration plan for the burnt moor on 7 October 2019, three months after the fires were contained. The plan envisages spreading fourteen hundred tonnes of cut heather as seed by helicopter in the winter, replanting sphagnum plugs from spring 2020, and blocking gullies to rewet the peat. The water company's interest is plain: the burnt catchment discharged discoloured water into the reservoir chain throughout August 2019, raising treatment costs. The wildlife trust hopes the curlew will return within five years. The public inquiry into the fires, called by the county's council leaders, opens on 12 November 2019, with the camping byelaws and the fire service's resourcing both on its agenda. Fundraising for the restoration, seeded with a hundred thousand pounds from the disaster appeal opened on 30 July 2019, stands at double that sum. The peat still smoulders in two places, a fire officer said at the plan's launch, but the rain now leads the fight.
