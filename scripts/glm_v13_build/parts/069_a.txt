[1] Title: Corriefell Survey Published, Day: May 15, 1966 Content: The water power board has published the long-awaited survey of the Corriefell catchment, dated 14 May 1966, recommending a dam and tunnel scheme of seventy megawatts. The survey, two years in the field, measures flows at four gauging stations and puts the annual yield at a firm figure the board calls comfortable for industrial supply. The board's chairman said the enabling act received assent would be sought in the next session of the legislature, and that drawings are already at an advanced stage. Objections are expected from the estate whose fishing water would be drawn down, and from the parish whose churchyard lies near the proposed headpond margin. The board's engineers gave evidence yesterday to the county's development committee, showing models of the intake tower. Opposition speakers questioned the costings' allowance for rock excavation, citing the difficulties met on the northern scheme of the 1950s. The board undertook to deposit full drawings at the county hall for public inspection from Monday.
[2] Title: Work Begins on Corriefell Dam, Day: April 10, 1968 Content: The first blasting at the Corriefell site was fired yesterday morning, marking the day dam work began, 9 April 1968, eight months after the enabling act received royal assent on 3 August 1967. The contractor's village of forty huts is complete, and the aerial ropeway to the quarry is being strung. The board's resident engineer said the diversion tunnel will be driven first, with the breakthrough expected before the end of 1970. Hiring has filled four hundred of the five hundred and twenty posts, and a recruiting office opens in the county town next week. The fishing estate has withdrawn its objection on terms that include a hatchery below the tailrace, details of which were announced with the start of work. The parish churchyard margin has been realigned by agreement, and the remains from the affected ground reinterred in consecrated land above the headpond line. The board repeated its pledge that the first turbine would be spinning by the winter of 1971.
[3] Title: Corriefell Scheme Officially Opened, Day: July 1, 1972 Content: The Queen's lieutenant opened the Corriefell scheme yesterday, 30 June 1972, in showery weather before a crowd of two thousand. The ceremony completes a programme whose milestones came thick and fast: the tunnel broke through on 22 June 1970, the first turbine began spinning on 17 November 1971, and the scheme was fully commissioned on 28 February 1972 after the second turbine passed its load tests. The official opening, delayed from the spring at the board's request, thus follows the technical completion by four months. The reservoir reached top water level in April, and the hatchery below the tailrace received its first ova in May. The contractor's village is being dismantled, and the aerial ropeway's timber is sold to a fencing firm. The board's chairman told the crowd that the scheme's power would be carried to the industrial belt by the new line now being surveyed, and he paid tribute to the five hundred and twenty workers, whose bonus for early completion was announced the same evening. No further works in the catchment were announced at the ceremony, and the board's programme beyond the opening remains unstated.

[1] Title: Blackmuir Conversion Announced, Day: January 21, 1974 Content: The central generating board has announced the conversion of Blackmuir power station from coal to dual firing, the announcement made on 20 January 1974 at a briefing for the county's local authorities. The conversion, to be executed without a full station closure, will allow the burning of the new low-sulphur coal alongside imported fuel oil. The board's statement sets no date for the work, but its appendix envisages the principal items being complete within thirty months. The station's two hundred and forty workers were told of the plan at separate meetings, and their union's district secretary said afterwards that jobs appear secure. Local authorities asked about the effect on the coal rakes, which have run three a day since the 1950s; the board said the traffic would fall but not cease. The announcement follows the national review of generating capacity published last autumn. No mention was made of the flue equipment, whose specification is still being drafted.
[2] Title: Scrubbers Fitted at Blackmuir, Day: October 6, 1976 Content: The flue gas scrubbers were fitted at Blackmuir on 5 October 1976, the principal item of the conversion announced in January 1974, and the station's chimneys will be clear of visible plume by the month's end. The fitting, done during a nine-week outage, completes the dual-firing installation. The board confirmed that the next item in the station's programme is the rebuilding of the main chimney, weakened by two decades of acidic flue gas, for which scaffolding orders have been placed. The chimney was rebuilt during the following programme year, the work finishing on 12 March 1978, and the board's annual report prints a photograph of the new stack. The station's output during the outage was made good by the northern hydro stations. The union's safety committee inspected the scrubber galleries last week and found them satisfactory. Local anglers reported the river below the station running clearer than in any autumn within memory.
[3] Title: Interconnector Energised at Blackmuir, Day: May 27, 1981 Content: The cross-country interconnector was energised at 09:30 yesterday, 26 May 1981, linking Blackmuir to the southern grid and completing a line first projected in the survey dispatched on 7 September 1978. The energisation follows two years of tower erection across the moors, contested at three public inquiries. Power now flows in both directions, and the board says Blackmuir's surplus will firm the southern system's winter margin. The station was recertified for the higher output on 14 August 1981 after boiler inspections, the certificate being signed at the board's autumn meeting. The interconnector survey of 1978 had recommended the western route eventually adopted, rejecting a coastal alignment that would have crossed the ornithological reserve. The line's switching stations are manned by eighteen new recruits, the first of whom completed their training in the spring. A formal opening ceremony for the line is not planned, the board considering the energisation itself sufficient mark of the work.

[1] Title: Blackout Strikes Western Grid, Day: December 12, 1984 Content: The blackout struck the western grid at 17:41 on 11 December 1984, cutting supply to four hundred thousand homes through the darkest hour of the evening peak. Power was restored to the last districts at 22:07 on 14 December 1984, after three days of rolling reconnections. The cause, according to the board's first statement, was a protection fault at the northern switching station, which tripped three circuits in cascade. The minister has ordered a full inquiry under a senior engineering judge. Hospitals ran on standby plant, and the water works lost pressure in the high districts. Trams stood in the streets, and the evening's theatre performances were cancelled. The board's chairman apologised to consumers and said compensation would follow the inquiry's findings. Opposition members called for the resignation of the board's operations director, a call the government declined to support. Stocks of candles sold out across the region by the second morning.
[2] Title: Blackout Inquiry Opens, Day: January 23, 1985 Content: The inquiry into the December blackout opened on 22 January 1985 in the county hall, the senior engineering judge sitting alone. Counsel for the board began by reading the timeline of the night of 11 December 1984, from the first protection fault at 17:41 to the restoration completed on 14 December. The inquiry is expected to sit for a fortnight, hearing evidence from protection engineers, the operations director, and consumers' representatives. The judge has asked for the maintenance records of the northern switching station to be produced in full, after counsel for the consumers noted a gap in the testing schedule. The board's witnesses conceded that the protection scheme had not been fully reviewed since its installation. Sitting days were fixed through February, and the judge indicated his report would follow within months rather than a year. A press room has been set aside in the hall's old library.
[3] Title: Wind Pilot Feeds Western Grid, Day: February 3, 1987 Content: The experimental wind turbine at Blackmuir edge first fed the grid on 2 February 1987, eighteen weeks after it was erected on 19 September 1986. The pilot, the board's first venture into wind, follows the blackout affair: the inquiry report was published on 9 July 1985, and its recommendations were implemented on 3 March 1986, including the replacement of the northern switching station's protection scheme. Power was restored to the last blackout district on 14 December 1984, but the affair's true end is marked by the implementation date. The turbine, of two hundred and fifty kilowatts, will run a two-year assessment, and an expansion study was commissioned on 30 October 1987 to weigh a cluster of machines on the same ridge. The pilot's first month has produced sixty per cent of its rated energy, a figure the board's engineers called encouraging for the ridge's exposure. The study's findings are not expected before the autumn, and no commitment to further machines has been made.
