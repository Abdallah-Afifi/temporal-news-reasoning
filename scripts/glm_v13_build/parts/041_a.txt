[1] Title: Sorve Basin Mass Vaccination Drive Begins, Day: April 13, 1967 Content: The largest medical mobilisation in the region's history began yesterday, 12 April 1967, as some four thousand vaccinators set out from district depots for the villages of the Sorve river basin. The campaign, planned over the winter, aims to reach every resident within eighteen months using the new bifurcated needle. A central surveillance unit was formed on 30 June 1967 to compile weekly case returns, its creation brought forward by an outbreak along the middle river. To encourage reporting, a reward scheme offering a sack of grain for every confirmed case brought forward was introduced on 15 October 1967, and health posts reported a threefold rise in notifications within a month. The programme's director, Dr. Halima Corr, said the jet injectors would move to the northern tributaries once the harvest ends. Logistics officers described the petrol supply as the campaign's chief constraint, with lorries queued three deep at the depot pumps. The basin's last recorded epidemic, in 1958, killed an estimated six thousand people, and the memory of it has smoothed the vaccinators' reception in most villages.
[2] Title: Last Smallpox Case Found in Sorve City, Day: March 15, 1972 Content: Health officials confirmed yesterday that the case detected on 9 March 1972 in Sorve City's dock quarter is the basin's last known case of smallpox. The outbreak that produced it began in the last week of February, and a containment team was flown to the city on 28 February 1972, vaccinating eleven thousand contacts in a ring around the infected streets within nine days. The patient, a stevedore, has recovered and was released from the isolation hospital on Monday. Surveillance officers have doubled the reward for reporting cases, and a house-to-house search is under way in the dock wards to confirm that no further infections simmer undetected. The director of the campaign since its launch in April 1967 credited the surveillance unit's weekly returns with pinpointing the outbreak within days of the first rash. Import restrictions on second-hand bedding from the delta ports have been tightened as a precaution. Officers caution that two full years of searches without a case must pass before the basin can be declared free of the disease.
[3] Title: Basin Declared Free of Smallpox, Day: November 22, 1977 Content: The international certification commission, which convened on 5 October 1977 to examine five years of records, has declared the Sorve river basin free of smallpox, the declaration being signed on 21 November 1977 at a ceremony in the regional capital. The declaration follows the exhaustive searches that succeeded the last case of March 1972 and the reward scheme that by 1974 had itself become a fixture of village life. The commission examined specimen collections in three capitals and took evidence from district officers over six weeks. Routine surveillance was formally stood down on 30 November 1979, the last weekly return being bound into the archive the same day, though the reward notices will remain on health-post walls at reduced rates for a further period. Dr. Halima Corr, who directed the campaign from its first day in April 1967, told the ceremony that the basin's example had hastened neighbouring territories' programmes. A commemorative ward at the Sorve City isolation hospital, named for the stevedore patient of 1972 who died in an unrelated accident three years later, is to be endowed from the campaign's unspent funds.

[1] Title: Greenfield Hospital Plans Approved, Day: May 19, 1969 Content: The regional health board has marked the start of work on the Greenfield district hospital, eighteen months after its plans were approved on 2 April 1968. The site, a sloping field outside the town, was cleared of its old orchard walls by 14 October 1968, and yesterday, 18 May 1969, the foundation stone was laid by the board's chairman in a brief ceremony. The contract, let to a consortium of two local firms, provides for six hundred beds in four wings, with the east wing scheduled for completion first. The architect's model, displayed in the town hall, shows wards arranged in fingers to catch the valley light, a scheme the matron praised at the laying ceremony. Work is expected to employ three hundred men at peak. The board's treasurer warned that the programme depends on annual exchequer releases, and that slippage of a single quarter would push completion beyond the planned date. Local practitioners have been assured of consulting rooms in the outpatient block, ending their long dependence on converted shopfronts.
[2] Title: First Patients Enter Greenfield East Wing, Day: August 15, 1972 Content: The east wing of the Greenfield district hospital received its handover certificate on 30 June 1972, and the first patients were admitted on 14 August 1972, thirteen weeks behind the date projected at the 1969 foundation ceremony. The delay, blamed on a strike at the boiler works, has cost the consortium its early-completion bonus. The wing opens with two hundred and forty beds in general medicine and surgery, and its cardiac unit will open on 9 January 1973 once its nursing complement completes orientation, the unit's monitoring equipment having already been installed and tested. The matron reported that recruitment has filled all but eleven posts. Transfer of patients from the aging workhouse infirmary begins next week and should be complete by the autumn. The regional board's chairman, laying open day, said the finger-ward design had already reduced lift journeys by a third compared with the old buildings. Bus services to the site have been doubled at off-peak hours for visiting.
[3] Title: Maternity Extension Opens at Greenfield, Day: September 26, 1975 Content: The maternity extension at Greenfield district hospital, given the go-ahead on 20 March 1974 after a spirited campaign by the town's GPs, opened on 25 September 1975 and welcomed its first births within hours. The extension adds a seventy-cot unit and a midwifery school, and completes the hospital's build-out begun with the 1968 plan approval. The official opening yesterday was performed by the regional minister, who also unveiled the commemorative stone recording the foundation ceremony of 18 May 1969. The board confirmed that the cardiac unit, open since January 1973, is to gain a second theatre next year. Building of the extension employed a peak of one hundred and forty men, and the consortium says it finished two weeks early, recovering something of the reputation lost to the east wing delays of 1972. The workhouse infirmary, emptied of patients since the 1972 transfers, is to be demolished in the spring. Mothers from the outlying valleys will be accommodated in a new annex paid for by the league of friends.

[1] Title: Measles Cases Surface in Millbrook, Day: February 8, 1983 Content: Eight children in Millbrook's eastern districts were reported to the medical officer on 7 February 1983 with the characteristic rash, and three more cases were confirmed by yesterday evening. The outbreak, the largest in the county since 1974, is centred on two primary schools whose pupils share a bus route. On 21 February 1983 the county closed both schools for a fortnight, a step last taken during the 1957 influenza winter. The medical officer has written to all family doctors asking for immediate notification of suspected cases, and health visitors have begun tracing contacts. A request for emergency vaccine has been telegraphed to the central stores. The district's immunisation rate, standing at sixty-two per cent, has long been flagged as vulnerable by the regional epidemiologist, whose 1981 memorandum warned that Millbrook was an outbreak waiting to happen. Hospital paediatric beds stand at eighty per cent occupancy. Parents queuing at the health centre yesterday reported waits of two hours for advice, and the local radio has opened a phone-in for concerned families.
[2] Title: Vaccine Arrives as Millbrook Epidemic Peaks, Day: May 16, 1983 Content: The emergency batch of measles vaccine, delivered to Millbrook on 30 March 1983 after a fortnight's delay in cold-chain certification, has already reached two-thirds of the susceptible children, the health authority reports. The epidemic peaked in the second week of April with ninety-four notifications in seven days, and the closure of the two eastern schools, in force since 21 February, was lifted at half-term. Mass vaccination clinics began on 15 May 1983 in the sports hall, immunising six hundred children on the first day, and will run each weekend through the summer. The medical officer said the 1981 memorandum's warning of low uptake had now been vindicated twice over, and that the authority would mount a catch-up programme in the autumn term. Complications have kept eleven children in hospital, one of them in the infectious diseases unit with pneumonia, but no deaths have occurred. The authority has ordered additional refrigeration for the health centre, whose single cold cabinet failed twice during the April rush, and a review of the cold chain has been promised.
[3] Title: Millbrook Measles Outbreak Declared Over, Day: October 1, 1983 Content: The Millbrook measles outbreak was declared over on 30 September 1983, forty-two days after the last confirmed case, in accordance with the conventional twice-the-incubation rule. In all, nine hundred and six cases were notified, with fourteen children hospitalised and none died. A follow-up campaign to lift the district's immunisation rate above ninety per cent was launched on 12 March 1984, using the sports hall clinics that had served the emergency, and by June had reached five thousand children. The authority's review of the episode, whose report was published on 30 June 1985, criticises the fortnight's delay in the vaccine's cold-chain certification and the single cold cabinet's failures, and recommends standby vaccine stocks at district level. The schools' fortnight-long closure of February 1983 is judged to have contributed little to control, the epidemic having already peaked before reopening. The 1981 memorandum is cited throughout as the warning the authority failed to act upon. The regional epidemiologist has been invited to address the authority in the autumn, and the two eastern schools have made immunisation a condition of their new enrolment registers.
