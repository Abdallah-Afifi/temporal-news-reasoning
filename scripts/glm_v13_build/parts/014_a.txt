[1] Title: Mills Combine in Million-Pound Merger, Day: February 20, 1902 Content: The announced merger of the four Ellis mills with the Cardrew group, published on 19 February 1902, creates the largest cotton combine in the district, thirty-one thousand spindles under one board. The terms give Ellis shareholders debentures at four per cent and a cash option on a third of their holdings. The combine's prospectus, issued the same week, promises economies in buying and selling and the retirement of the older mules for ring frames. The mill operatives' union has asked for assurances on wages; the masters reply that the merger is a matter of capital, not labour. The district's other mill owners read the prospectus with attention, three of them said to be preparing answers of their own. The bank's position, historically with Cardrew, extends in the merger to the Ellis overdrafts. The first general meeting of the combined company is called for 15 April 1902. The Ellis brothers, whose father built the first mill in 1861, take seats on the new board and the chairmanship goes to Cardew's son-in-law. The trade press calculates the combine's capital at one million one hundred thousand pounds.
[2] Title: Combine's First Results Below Promise, Day: March 17, 1905 Content: The combined mills' third annual accounts, published 16 March 1905, show profits of forty-one thousand pounds against the prospectus's promised sixty, and the debenture interest has been met only by borrowing. The ring-frame conversion, begun in 1903 at the Derwent mill, has overrun its cost by half and displaced two hundred mule spinners, whose union has called a strike ballot for 25 March 1905. The cash option of 1902, it emerges, was taken by so many Ellis shareholders that the combine has paid interest on its own capital ever since. The board's chairman, Cardew's son-in-law, has survived a vote of no confidence at the adjourned meeting of 10 March 1905 by the chairman's casting vote. The other three mill owners who prepared answers in 1902 have merged with each other instead, and their leaner combine is quoted at a premium. The operatives' ballot returned action on 22 March 1905; the strike began 3 April 1905 and ended, defeated, on 19 May 1905. The Derwent conversion was finished in the strike's quiet weeks.
[3] Title: Combine Absorbed by Its Rival, Day: June 12, 1907 Content: The Cardrew-Ellis combine has passed into the hands of its rival, the boards announced on 11 June 1907, the shareholders of 1902 receiving debenture stock at sixty per cent of its face and the equity nothing. The absorbing company, the lean combine of the three who merged in 1903, takes the mills as going concerns and closes four of the eleven at once — Derwent among them, its ring frames barely three years old. The strike's defeated operatives are scattered, the union's district secretary emigrating in the autumn. The chairman who survived by casting vote resigned in January 1907, his place filled by a receiver's nominee until the sale. The trade press's post-mortem, published 15 June 1907, dates the failure to the merger's first week: the cash option, it says, mortgaged the future to buy the past. The Ellis brothers, their debentures cut, retire to the coast. The bank, whole, extends to the victors. One mill, the original Ellis of 1861, is sold to a furniture manufacturer and runs yet.

=== PASSAGE P2 ===
[1] Title: Tramway Takeover Battle Opens, Day: October 11, 1921 Content: The British Electric Traction group's offer for the municipal-adjacent Corvedan Tramways, posted to shareholders on 10 October 1921, values the company at four hundred thousand pounds and opens the district's first takeover battle. The tramways, horse-drawn to 1899 and electric since, carry forty-one million passengers a year on twenty-six miles of track. The offer is opposed by the board, whose chairman denounces the financiers at a depot meeting, and by the corporation, which holds a quarter of the shares and fears the fares. The omnibus men, petrol's partisans, are said to be behind the bid, the trams' wires to come down within five years, though the bidder denies it. The union, whose members' jobs hang on the rails, asks the corporation to nationalise rather than see the takeover through. The shareholders' meeting called for 1 November 1921 will be the first test. The offer's closing date is 30 November 1921. The tramways' last accounts, to June 1921, show a dividend of five and a half per cent.
[2] Title: Takeover Complete, Wires to Stay Five Years, Day: January 24, 1923 Content: The Corvedan Tramways passed to the BET group on 23 January 1923, the corporation's holding having been bought at a premium after the board's resistance collapsed in the autumn of 1922. The guarantees extracted in the sale, published in full, are: the wires to stand until at least 1928, the depot workforce's wages to be maintained for three years, and eleven new cars to be built at the district's own works, the order confirmed 2 February 1923. The chairman who resisted is retired with the thanks of the line. The first economy announced is not the rails but the ticket staff: conductors to be retrained as drivers and the surplus found clerical work, the company promising no compulsory measures. The omnibus men's partisans got their petrol after all, but as feeders to the rails, not their replacements — the first bus routes, announced 15 March 1923, run to the tram termini and meet the cars. The shareholders' premium, the trade press notes, cost the district its independence.
[3] Title: Tramways' Dividend Restored, Wires Still Standing, Day: April 20, 1926 Content: The Corvedan tramways' accounts to December 1925, published 19 April 1926, show the dividend back to five per cent after the lean takeover years, and the new cars of 1923-24 are credited with the custom they won. The wires guaranteed to 1928 stand on all twenty-six miles, and the company's chairman — BET's area man since 1923 — told the annual meeting that the guarantee would be honoured and perhaps outlived. The conductors' retraining closed in June 1924 with no compulsory redundancies recorded, the clerical promise kept by attrition's patience. The feeder buses of March 1923 have multiplied to eight routes, and the general manager's report, read at the same meeting, puts the combined journeys at fifty-two million, the trams' share held at forty-one million still. The depot workforce, eight hundred and sixty at the takeover, stands at eight hundred and forty. A shed fire on 14 February 1926, put out in an hour, is the year's only excitement. The corporation, its premium spent on a swimming bath, watches from the gallery.

=== PASSAGE P3 ===
[1] Title: Shipyard Gives Notice to Eight Hundred, Day: November 4, 1930 Content: The Harrow and Lune shipyard gave notice on 3 November 1930 to eight hundred of its two thousand four hundred men, the largest single day's redundancy the yard has known. The order book, full in 1928, holds two tramps and a collier, enough work to the spring. The shipbuilding crisis, the trade press says, is now general, tonnage launched on the coast this year at a third of 1928's. The yard's directors met the men's committee on 31 October 1930 and offered short time as the alternative; the committee, split, took the notices. The union lodge's emergency fund stands at four hundred pounds. The town's relief committee, founded in the dock strike of 1912 and dormant since, reconvened on 5 November 1930 in the mechanics' institute. The yard's crane driver, forty years in the harness, was among the notices and among those who refused to believe them. The directors' statement speaks of the world's markets and asks the town's patience. The bank's position, the town speculates, is the true cause.
[2] Title: Last Keel Laid as Yard Falls Silent, Day: March 10, 1932 Content: The last keel in the Harrow and Lune yard was laid on 9 March 1932, a small coaster for a Norwegian account, and when she is off the ways in June the gates will close. The eight hundred notices of November 1930 were followed by six hundred more in the spring of 1931, and the remaining four hundred finish the coaster and go. The crane driver retired, harnessed and pensioned, on 31 December 1931, the yard's one gesture. The relief committee's soup kitchen, opened 12 January 1931, feeds eleven hundred daily. The Norwegian coaster's account, the town notes, was taken at a loss to keep the ways warm, and the trade press calls the launch of June 1932 the last the coast will see for years. The lodge's fund went in February 1931; the men's teaching staff in the institute's evening classes are largely yard men now, teaching ship's arithmetic to boys with no ships. The directors' last statement, 8 March 1932, regrets and thanks. The crane stands as it stood, weathered to the colour of the sky.
[3] Title: Yard Reopens Under New Owners, Day: September 17, 1934 Content: The Harrow and Lune yard reopened on 17 September 1934 under new owners, a Tyneside group that bought the works from the bank in April 1934 for a fifth of its 1930 value. The first keel, a stern trawler for the fleet at the mouth, was laid 15 September 1934, and three hundred and fifty of the old men signed on in the first week, the crane among them — not the pensioner of 1931, dead that winter, but his apprentice of the closing years. The relief committee's kitchen closed on 30 June 1934, its last recipients signed on at the gate. The new works' manager told the men the trawlers would be the first of twelve, and the order book by December 1934 held five. The institute's evening classes, the ship's arithmetic of the idle years, are credited by the manager with the yard's surprising output. The Norwegian coaster of 1932, the town's pride of that bleak June, was lost with all hands off Iceland in 1933, her bell given to the institute where it hangs. The gates, the town says, were never locked, only shut.
