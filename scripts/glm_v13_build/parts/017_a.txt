[1] Title: Milk Board Begins Collections in March, Day: January 28, 1938 Content: The milk marketing board's district scheme, approved by the ministers in December, begins collections on 4 March 1938, and the town's creamery is being rebuilt to take the whole of the vale's morning and evening yield. The scheme's promise is a guaranteed price, threepence a gallon winter and summer, against the auction's zigzag, and the farmers' federation voted for it 611 to 94 on 21 January 1938 after two years of argument. The independents, forty of them, hold out for their own rounds in the town, and the board says it will not compel them. The creamery's new silo, the first in the county, was topped out on 17 January 1938 and holds forty thousand gallons. The railway has promised a daily milk train from the new platform, its first run set for the scheme's first morning. The town's milk retailers, twenty-six of them, met on 25 January 1938 and resolved to watch the scheme's prices before joining anything. The board's district manager, a creamery man from the west, takes up his post on 14 February 1938.
[2] Title: Milk Train Derailment Tests the Scheme, Day: November 10, 1938 Content: The milk train's derailment at the junction on 8 November 1938, eight waggons of the vale's morning churning scattered, has tested the scheme's first winter and, the district manager says, proved it. The guarantee held: the farmers were paid on the Friday as if the train had run, the board absorbing the loss. The creamery's silo, its forty thousand gallons, gave two days' grace while the line was cleared, and the town's rounds were kept by the independents' forty floats, the holdouts of January become the scheme's rescuers. The railway's inquiry, opened 9 November 1938, blames a broken rail, and the board has asked for the junction's speed to be cut. The scheme's accounts to October, published this week, show the guaranteed price paid in full and a small surplus, and the federation's meeting of 5 November 1938 voted to admit the independents on the same terms, none now holding out. The retailers' watch of January has ended in merger: eighteen of the twenty-six have taken the board's wholesale terms.
[3] Title: War Shadows the Creamery, Day: October 2, 1939 Content: The milk scheme's second year begins under the war's new rules: the ministry of food took control of all milk sales on 29 September 1939, and the board's district machinery, silo, train and guarantee, has become the ministry's local instrument. The guaranteed price of 1938 survives, the minister confirming threepence with a farthing added for the winter. The milk train, thinned to five days by the fuel order of 22 September 1939, runs still, its waggons repainted. The derailment's broken rail has been replaced along the whole junction, the speed cut of 1938 now permanent. The district manager, western creamery man and now the ministry's local officer, has billeted two of his chums from the western creamery in the creamery's own loft. The vale's yield, the accounts to March 1939 show, rose eleven per cent in the scheme's first year, the federation's chairman crediting the guarantee's courage. The independents' floats, the scheme's rescuers of November 1938, are requisitioned on 1 October 1939 for the ambulances' conversion, the rounds to be walked.

[1] Title: Root Field Committee Formed for Digging, Day: August 20, 1940 Content: The county's war agricultural committee, meeting on 19 August 1940, has ordered the ploughing of the vale's third pasture, eight hundred acres of grazing that has known nothing but cows, and the growing of roots and cereals in its place. The order falls on forty-one farms, and the committee's field officers, six of them, begin their visits on 26 August 1940. The farmers' federation protests the loss of the dairy's keep; the committee answers with the ministry's arithmetic, men and milk against horses and bread. The digging itself is to be done by the new tractors, two Fordsons arriving on 15 September 1940, and by the prisoners of war, a camp of two hundred proposed for the estate's walled garden. The pasture's old clover, twenty years undisturbed, turns under black and sour, the older men shaking their heads. The first sowing, swedes for the winter, is set for the last week of September. The committee's chairman, a barley man from the hills, told the meeting that the vale would thank them when the bread ran short, and the vale, politely, waited.
[2] Title: First Harvest of the New Fields, Day: October 15, 1941 Content: The new fields' first harvest is in: four thousand tons of swedes and mangolds, two thousand of barley, lifted and carted by the end of September 1941, and the committee's harvest report of 10 October 1941 calls it the county's best conversion. The prisoners' camp, its two hundred risen to three hundred and fifty, did the lifting, the walled garden's huts overflowing into the estate's stable yard. The Fordsons, two in 1940, are six, and one of them has already been to pieces and back, its gearbox rebuilt by the estate's smith in a week. The dairy's protest of 1940 has its answer in the milk figures: the vale's yield, eleven per cent up in the scheme's first year, held level through the conversion, the roots replacing the clover's keep. The bread did not run short, the chairman's prophecy of August 1940 quietly forgotten, but the swedes fed the camp and the county's hogs, and the barley went to the breweries on licence. The committee's order for 1942, posted 13 October 1941, takes the fourth pasture, and the vale, its arithmetic now believed, raised no protest at all.
[3] Title: Pastures Back to Grass, the Committee Winds Up, Day: May 12, 1947 Content: The war agricultural committee, its last order signed 30 April 1947, winds itself up this month, and the vale's third and fourth pastures are going back to grass under the ministry's reinstatement grants, the first sowing of the new ley on 5 May 1947. The conversion's ledger, the committee's final report of 2 May 1947 records: seven years' roots and cereals, four thousand tons a year at the peak, no milk lost, two hundred and fifty acres of the vale's clay underdrained for nothing, the drainage's worth to outlast the cereals by decades. The prisoners' camp, its numbers fallen from three hundred and fifty to forty, closes in June, the last of them repatriated through the port. The Fordsons, six and worn, pass to the farms at the ministry's prices, four staying in the vale. The chairman, barley man of the hills, told the winding-up dinner of 10 May 1947 that the vale had thanked them after all, and the vale, dining, let it stand. The dairy herd, sixty cows the fewer at the conversion's trough, is back to strength and the milk train, thinned and thinned again, runs three days.

[1] Title: Cannery Announces Expansion and Contracts, Day: March 18, 1958 Content: The fruit and vegetable cannery announced on 17 March 1958 an expansion doubling its season's throughput, and with it contracts for two thousand acres of the vale's peas and plums. The cannery, in the town's station yard since 1931, has canned the vale's soft fruit for the London trade; the expansion adds a pea vinery station at the crossroads and a plum receival yard, both promised for the 1959 season. The contracts, offered to the vale's ninety growers at fixed tonnages and fixed prices, were taken up by sixty-one by the first week of April, the federation's chairman calling the terms the best since the war. The cannery's manager, a Londoner six years in the town, told the growers' meeting of 20 March 1958 that the vinery station would lift the pea harvest's bottleneck, the vining now done in the fields by travelling machines. The plum growers, the vale's orchards aging, asked for grubbing grants and got promises of representation. The season's first fleet of canning lorries is contracted from the milk scheme's old float garage. The expansion's cost, the board's statement says, is three hundred thousand pounds.
[2] Title: Cannery's First Season Under New Capacity, Day: September 23, 1959 Content: The cannery's first season at the doubled capacity ended on 19 September 1959, the pea campaign of June and the plum campaign of August-September both through the new works, and the manager's report, published 22 September 1959, calls it a conversion equal to the farm one. The vinery station at the crossroads, opened 8 June 1959, took the two thousand acres' peas in nineteen days, the fields' travelling machines replaced by the station's two viners working day and night. The plum receival yard, opened a fortnight later than promised on 20 July 1959, saw the orchards' biggest crop in a decade, and the grubbing grants asked for in 1958 have arrived, the ministry's scheme of March 1959 paying for the old trees' replacement at forty an acre. The growers under contract rose from sixty-one to seventy-eight, the fixed prices honoured through a wet August that would once have broken the auction. The canning lorries from the float garage ran their second season. The report's one shadow: the London trade's merger, its buying now one office instead of three, the terms for 1960 to be fought for.
[3] Title: London Terms Beat the Cannery, the Vale Buys It, Day: November 30, 1965 Content: The cannery, its London trade's merged buying office squeezing the margins for five years since 1959, has been sold to the growers themselves: the cooperative of the vale's seventy-eight contract growers completed the purchase on 26 November 1965 for one hundred and ten thousand pounds, the float garage's lorries and the crossroads viners included. The manager of 1958's expansion, the Londoner turned townsman, stays on two years to hand over, his 1966 retirement announced with the sale. The co-op's first act, decided 29 November 1965, is the plum yard's extension and a canning line for the vale's new blackcurrants, sixty acres planted under the co-op's own advance. The grubbing grants of 1959-63 renewed the orchards, and the young trees are bearing now. The London office, the co-op's chairman told the meeting, buys from one cannery as cheaply as another, so the cannery had better be the vale's own. The station yard's works, 1931's founding and 1958's doubling, pass to the growers whole, and the season of 1966, the co-op's first, will tell.
